\title[EMA-Det]{EMA-Det: Hardware-Aware Edge Verification for Two-Stage UAV Detection}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Enes Mert} \sur{Ayd{\i}n}}\email{23050211025@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Osman} \sur{Tokluo{\u{g}}lu}}\email{otokluoglu@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Enver} \sur{{\c{C}}avu{\c{s}}}}\email{ecavus@aybu.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering}, \orgname{Ankara Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m Beyaz{\i}t University}, \orgaddress{\city{Ankara}, \country{T{\"u}rkiye}}}

\abstract{Detecting Low-Slow-Small (LSS) unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) on edge devices usually takes two stages: a lightweight proposal stage nominates candidate regions, and a verification network checks them under tight compute and memory budgets. This paper presents EMA-Det, a hardware-aware verification network for that second stage. It combines a first layer initialized from Laplacian/Sobel kernels with two early-exit gates that let confident background regions skip most of the network. On a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset, under a controlled protocol ($N{=}5$ seeds, 150 epochs), EMA-Det reaches $96.08\pm0.47\%$ verification recall (target accepted, box not checked), above MobileNetV3-Small ($94.05\pm1.39\%$) and ShuffleNetV2 ($93.80\pm1.11\%$) trained identically. At a $5\%$ false-positive rate it accepts $96.3\pm1.0\%$ of the targets, against $88.4\pm2.8\%$ for a COCO-pretrained and $79.5\pm3.1\%$ for a from-scratch YOLOv8n, a full-frame detector with 19 times the FLOPs and much higher mAP. Its localization is weaker (box recall at IoU\,0.5 is $71.7\%$, no better than YOLOv8n's): it is a better verifier, not a better detector. Mean average precision (mAP) alone is an unreliable guide to verifier quality: system-level recall, $R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, is bounded by the weaker factor. We also give a static hardware-resource analysis and a synthesized, resource-verified field-programmable gate array (FPGA) implementation (Zynq-7020) of the first block. On the Anti-UAV410 thermal-infrared (IR) benchmark, a verifier trained on electro-optical (EO) images transfers zero-shot with precision intact, but recall drops to $80.12$--$86.29\%$ and mAP@0.5 to $39.38$--$52.05\%$. Trained from scratch on thermal data, EMA-Det reaches mAP@0.5 of $89.52\pm0.93\%$ and recall of $91.64\pm0.41\%$, which points to genuine domain shift rather than an architectural ceiling.}

\keywords{LSS UAV Detection, Region Verification, Early-Exit, FPGA, Edge Computing, Cross-Sensor Generalization}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\textit{This manuscript extends a paper published at IEEE SIU
2026~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf} with a controlled unified protocol
(Sections~\ref{sec:cbam} and~\ref{sec:leakage}), a decoupled spatial
bounding-box (bbox) head (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) and a
cross-sensor study (Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}).}

Low-Slow-Small (LSS) unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) pose an asymmetric
threat to civilian and military infrastructure and call for a fast,
reliable sense--decide--act loop~\cite{luo2021threat,aydin2025aads}.
Electro-optical (EO) sensors now typically deliver 4K ($3840\times2160$)
or more, which standard deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) cannot
process at full frame in real time on edge devices, because of bandwidth
and memory limits~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}. Fielded systems therefore
use a two-stage cascade~\cite{kiefer2023roi}: a first stage (radar
priors~\cite{nabati2019rrpn}, acoustic sensors~\cite{tegler2025sound}, or
motion and background subtraction) proposes candidate regions of interest
(RoIs), and a second stage crops small patches and verifies them.

Prior work usually adapts general-purpose detectors (the YOLO family,
SSD)~\cite{li2020object} to RoI
verification~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. We argue that RoI
verification is \emph{not} just a scaled-down detection problem. On edge
devices memory bandwidth is a tighter bottleneck than raw
compute~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}, and compressed YOLO variants run into
resource and energy--performance limits on field-programmable gate array
(FPGA)-class hardware~\cite{adiono2021low,zhang2022apply}. Full-frame
architectures squeezed into a micro-input regime also lose spatial
information through deep global pooling. And because total system recall is
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, every
false negative of the verifier is a miss for the whole system. Recall, more
than mAP, is therefore the critical metric for the second stage (though not
the only thing that decides system performance).

\paragraph{What ``hardware-aware'' means here.} Design choices follow the
constraints of the target FPGA (on-chip block RAM (BRAM), digital signal
processing (DSP) slices and memory bandwidth), not just floating-point
operations (FLOPs): (i)~standard instead of depthwise-separable convolutions,
which put more pressure on bandwidth in BRAM-limited fabrics; (ii)~a
streaming-friendly backbone whose first block needs only a 3-row line buffer;
(iii)~per-RoI early-exit gates, which suit a verifier that handles one RoI at
a time; and (iv)~fixed-point arithmetic checked with high-level synthesis.
Only (ii) for Block~1 and (iv) are verified in hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:hardware}); (i) and (iii) are design rationale.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1)~\emph{Recall-centred verifier design.} We treat RoI verification as the
second factor of $R_{\text{total}}$. Over $N{=}5$ seeds, a small purpose-built
network exceeds the verification recall of identically trained lightweight
backbones by about 2 points and, at a fixed false-alarm rate, that of YOLOv8n
by 8 to 17 points, with 19 times fewer FLOPs but weaker localization. We
report recall with and without a box-overlap requirement, across thresholds
and at a fixed false-positive rate (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).
(2)~\emph{Background-aware early exit.} Two confidence gates let
background candidates skip most of the network, cutting average FLOPs per
RoI by 12.5--21.2\% at a cost of 0.4--1.0 recall points
(Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}). (3)~\emph{A box head that keeps spatial
information.} Regressing the box from the pre-pooling feature map explains
most of the benefit that the Convolutional Block Attention Module
(CBAM)~\cite{woo2018cbam} appeared to give; on top of this head, CBAM
costs recall (Sections~\ref{sec:spatialbbox} and~\ref{sec:cbam}).
(4)~\emph{Hardware feasibility.} Block~1 is synthesized and co-simulated on a
Zynq-7020, and the scaling limits and a roadmap for the remaining blocks are
quantified (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}). We also release a deduplicated,
source-level-split dataset pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) and measure
EO$\to$thermal-IR transfer on Anti-UAV410 (Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Two-stage detection and efficient backbones.} Two-stage
detectors separate proposal from verification and trade latency for
accuracy relative to single-stage designs~\cite{ren2015faster}; cascades
refine localization over stages with increasing intersection-over-union
(IoU) thresholds~\cite{cai2018cascade}. UAV work follows the same
split~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. EMA-Det instead targets
the verification stage itself, through $R_{\text{total}} =
R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:intro}),
rather than shrinking a general detector. Mobile backbones cut FLOPs with
depthwise-separable or grouped convolutions~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3}
and channel shuffle~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}, but FPGA studies show that
these raise memory-bandwidth pressure on BRAM-constrained
fabrics~\cite{zhao2024fpga}. That is consistent with EMA-Det's shallow
backbone of standard convolutions (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), and FPGA
accelerator design is governed by the balance between compute and memory
bandwidth~\cite{zhang2015fpga}.

\paragraph{UAV and small-object detection.} Anti-UAV
detection and tracking has been widely
benchmarked~\cite{zhao2022antiuav,jiang2021antiuav,zhu2021visdrone}.
Seidaliyeva et al.~\cite{seidaliyeva2020drone} detect drones from motion
cues and a CNN classifier, and small-object detection is tackled, for
example, by sliced inference~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. These works mostly
evaluate full-frame detection by mAP, whereas EMA-Det targets the
verification stage of a cascade and selects models by recall.
Cross-modality UAV detection has been studied as EO/IR
fusion~\cite{sun2022dronevehicle} and as single-sensor thermal-IR
detection~\cite{antiuav410}; Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor} quantifies the
zero-shot EO$\to$thermal gap of our verifier.

\paragraph{Early-exit inference and attention.} Confidence-gated early exits
let easy inputs skip later
stages~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,liang2024implementation}
(surveyed in~\cite{rahmath2024earlyexit}), usually for classification without
a strong class prior; EMA-Det's gates (Section~\ref{sec:gates}) instead exploit
the background majority typical of RoI verification. The Convolutional Block
Attention Module (CBAM) recalibrates channel and spatial features at
negligible cost~\cite{woo2018cbam}, and stride-free downsampling preserves
small-object information that strided pooling discards~\cite{sunkara2022spdconv};
Sections~\ref{sec:cbam} and~\ref{sec:spatialbbox} reach a related conclusion
with a decoupled spatial bbox head instead of attention.

\section{EMA-Det: Architecture and Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System-Level Latency Model}\label{sec:latency}
In a two-stage system, total latency is the proposal time plus the
verification times of all candidates,
$T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{proposal}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RoI}}} T_{\text{verifier}}^{(i)}$.
A full-frame detector makes every patch, even empty sky, pay the full cost of
the network. EMA-Det's early exits keep the linear dependence on
$N_{\text{RoI}}$ but lower the average cost per candidate $\bar T_{\text{verifier}}$:
$T_{\text{total}}\approx T_{\text{proposal}}+N_{\text{RoI}}\,\bar T_{\text{verifier}}$.

\subsection{Laplacian/Sobel-Initialized Kernel Bank}\label{sec:init}
At $128\times128$ resolution, small targets carry little spatial
information. We initialize the weights $W_1$ of
the first convolution layer from a deterministic bank of Laplacian
($L$)~\cite{marr1980theory} and Sobel ($S$)~\cite{kanopoulos1988design}
operators:
\begin{equation}
W^{(0)}_{1,c} = \begin{cases}
K_c, & c \in \{1,\dots,K\} \\
R_c, & c \in \{K+1,\dots,C_{\text{out}}\}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
Here $K_c$ are the deterministic physical priors ($L$, $L_2$, $S_x$,
$S_y$ and their signed sums), in the spirit of hand-designed filter priors
in CNNs~\cite{luan2018gabor}, and $R_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ is a
random term, as in standard random initialization~\cite{he2015delving},
which keeps the layer free to learn beyond the fixed operators. The
weights are not frozen; they are updated like those of any other layer.
The motivation is an inductive-bias argument: known edge operators give a
prior on local gradient structure. Section~\ref{sec:cbam} tests it against random
initialization and finds no measurable gain. Figure~\ref{fig:featmaps} shows the
resulting Block~1 responses: the Laplacian and Sobel channels give clean edge
maps of a UAV and of a bird, and the trained kernels stay close to their priors
(cosine similarity $0.9998$ on average over the twelve fixed channels and five
seeds, minimum $0.9968$). An untrained random layer also responds to edges (last
column), so the figure illustrates the prior and does not prove a benefit.

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Block~1 (Section~\ref{sec:init})
turns $1\times128\times128$ into $16\times64\times64$; Blocks~2--4 reduce
this step by step to $128\times8\times8$; Block~5 fuses two parallel
dilated branches ($d{=}1,2$) into $128\times4\times4$; Block~6 expands it
to $256\times4\times4$. A verification head (global average pooling (GAP),
dropout $0.4$) produces a classification logit, and a separate spatial
branch (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) produces the 4-coordinate box
refinement.

CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} is \emph{not} part of the default forward path
(Section~\ref{sec:cbam}).

\subsection{Spatial Bounding-Box Head}\label{sec:spatialbbox}
Keeping spatial feature information for box regression, instead of
regressing from a globally pooled classification vector, is standard
practice in modern detectors~\cite{ren2015faster,liu2016ssd}.
An earlier configuration pooled before both branches and so discarded
spatial layout. Here, classification stays on the pooled vector while the
box head reads the $256\times4\times4$ feature map before pooling
(flattened into one linear layer). The legacy row of
Table~\ref{tab:cbam} reports a controlled $N{=}5$ ablation against the
pooled-vector configuration: the spatial head improves mAP@0.75 (mAP at an
IoU threshold of 0.75) by 5.7 points and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 2.9 points, for
only 15K extra parameters (${\sim}1\%$) and negligible FLOPs. Recall is
essentially unchanged and its standard deviation across seeds drops
($2.54\to0.47$). It is the default throughout this paper.

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}
The verification head combines a classification term and a box-regression
term (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, p) + \lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}(\hat{b}, b)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$ is the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}$ is the Complete-IoU (CIoU) loss~\cite{zheng2020distance},
$\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$ and $\lambda_{\text{box}}=2.0$. The early-exit
gates add their own BCE terms ($\lambda_{\text{gate1}}=0.3$,
$\lambda_{\text{gate2}}=0.4$) against the same binary label.

\subsection{Dynamic Early-Exit Gates}\label{sec:gates}
Following the confidence-gated early-exit idea
(e.g.\ BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}), Gates~1 and~2
(after Blocks~2 and~4) apply the threshold rule of Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}: a
candidate halts, classified as background with no box refinement, once
its confidence falls below $1-\tau$; otherwise it continues.
\begin{equation}
\text{Result} = \begin{cases}
\text{Background (halt)}, & \hat{y}_i \le (1-\tau) \\
C_{i+1}(F_i)\ (\text{continue}), & \hat{y}_i > (1-\tau)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}\label{sec:leakage}

The dataset comes from two public raw sources: photographs of rotary- and
fixed-wing LSS UAVs from a Kaggle collection~\cite{dasmehdixtr_kaggle}
(labeled, 80\% of the raw pool) and bird and empty-sky photographs from a
Roboflow Universe collection~\cite{mohsen_roboflow} (unlabeled hard
negatives, 20\%). Together they contain 8{,}793 photographs: 5{,}921
labeled UAV candidates and 2{,}872 negatives. Both collections were
scraped from the web and include frames extracted in sequence from videos,
so many photographs are near-duplicates. To keep the evaluation
uncontaminated, we compare $8\times8$ average hashes (Hamming distance
$\le 5$), cluster them with union-find and keep one representative per
cluster, preferring a labeled one, before patch extraction. This leaves 2{,}142
unique labeled and 887 unique sky photographs, split into train/val/test
(70\%/15\%/15\%) \emph{before} cropping, so every patch inherits the split of
its source photograph. From each photograph we crop one positive patch
($3\times$ context factor) and one hard-negative patch (same photograph, near
but not overlapping the UAV), giving 4{,}136 patches (2{,}842/662/632
train/val/test). Values from the conference version~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf}
came from a patch-level split without deduplication and are superseded.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All models are trained with AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), a 5-epoch
linear warmup, cosine annealing, batch size 16 and gradient clipping (norm
5.0), with early stopping (patience 20 on validation mAP@0.5, at most 150
epochs). All results, including the CBAM ablation, use $N{=}5$ seeds
$\{42, 7, 123, 2024, 31415\}$ under this same protocol. Augmentation uses
flips, brightness/contrast jitter, motion blur, noise, small rotations and
coarse dropout. We leave out Mosaic and MixUp because they destroy the
shape of small targets.

losing accuracy: depthwise-separable Blocks~4--6 (1.53M to 0.30M parameters,
423.1M to 265.6M FLOPs), Block~6 narrowed from 256 to 128 channels (0.93M
parameters, 404.2M FLOPs) and a multi-scale box head that also reads the
$32{\times}32$ Block~3 map (+0.19M parameters). A paired $t$-test over the
five matched seeds finds no significant difference from the default in any
metric (all $p>0.05$); the multi-scale head, which looked better in a
single-seed pilot, did not hold over five seeds. We keep standard
convolutions for the hardware reasons in Section~\ref{sec:intro}; in the
depthwise variant Gate~2 also sits after 95\% of the compute instead of
83\%, so early exit saves much less.

With the spatial bbox head, a paired $t$-test over the five matched seeds
finds no significant difference between configurations in mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75
or mAP@0.5:0.95 ($p=0.89$, $0.77$, $0.83$). CBAM still costs recall:
$93.23\pm1.37\%$ against $96.08\pm0.47\%$ (paired $t$-test, $p=0.017$). With
the legacy pooled-vector head, 2 of 5 CBAM seeds collapsed to 74--79\%
recall (std $8.85$); this is absent with the spatial head (worst run
90.8\%). CBAM's benefit there thus seems to have made up for spatial
information that pooling had discarded; once that is restored it adds no
localization benefit, so the final architecture leaves it out.

\textbf{Initialization.} The two initialization rows of Table~\ref{tab:cbam}
repeat EMA-Det with a random first layer and with the twelve fixed kernels
frozen (same seeds and protocol). The differences are within noise (paired
$t$-test, random initialization: mAP@0.5 $p=0.98$, recall $p=0.93$; frozen
kernels: $p=0.44$ and $0.26$), as is validation mAP@0.5 after 5, 10 and 20
epochs ($21.6/37.4/50.2\%$ against $23.2/39.8/49.3\%$ with random
initialization). The prior thus brings no measurable gain, and we make no
performance claim for it. The frozen channels
could be computed with shifts and additions instead of multipliers; we have not
synthesized this variant.

At a score threshold of 0.5, EMA-Det accepts $96.08\pm0.47\%$ of the targets,
against $69.94\pm1.64\%$ for a from-scratch and $77.91\pm2.82\%$ for a
COCO-pretrained YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}. This partly reflects the
threshold: at 0.1 YOLOv8n accepts $96.0\%$ and $97.5\%$, but at a precision of
$86.2\%$ and $88.4\%$ against $95.0\%$ for EMA-Det (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).
A fair comparison therefore fixes the false-alarm rate. At a $5\%$
false-positive rate, EMA-Det accepts $96.3\pm1.0\%$ of the targets, against
$88.4\pm2.8\%$ and $79.5\pm3.1\%$ (AUROC $0.987\pm0.002$ against
$0.978\pm0.007$ and $0.968\pm0.003$; Welch $t$-test over five seeds, $p<0.01$
except for the AUROC against the COCO-pretrained model, $p=0.047$), with 19
times fewer FLOPs. Localization is the opposite. With a box-overlap
requirement (Recall$^{\ddagger}$), EMA-Det reaches $71.7\%$, no better than the
COCO-pretrained YOLOv8n ($70.8\%$ at 0.5, $83.2\%$ at 0.1), and its
mAP@0.5:0.95 is far lower ($28.04\pm1.90\%$ against $49.59\pm0.45\%$ and
$54.10\pm1.48\%$), reflecting YOLOv8n's multi-scale, distribution-focal-loss
(DFL) localization. The detector's own validation recall ($73.75\pm1.86\%$,
$78.17\pm0.57\%$) is box-level at its best-F1 threshold and not comparable.
EMA-Det is thus the better verifier, not the better detector. Given
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, the acceptance
rate at a given false-alarm rate bounds system recall, and mAP does not predict it.

MobileNetV3-Small~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} and ShuffleNetV2
x0.5~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}, trained with the same head and protocol, are
closer. EMA-Det's AUROC exceeds theirs ($0.980\pm0.003$, $0.975\pm0.003$), and
at a $5\%$ false-positive rate it accepts $96.3\pm1.0\%$ of the targets against
$90.4\pm2.1\%$ and $88.2\pm2.9\%$ ($p<0.01$); their mAP is within the seed spread
of EMA-Det's. The price is computation: 423.1M FLOPs, 11 and 17 times those of
MobileNetV3-Small (38.0M) and ShuffleNetV2 (24.9M), which also have fewer
parameters. The depthwise variant (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}, 0.30M parameters) keeps an AUROC
of $0.989\pm0.002$, at 11 and 7 times their FLOPs. Early exit lowers EMA-Det's average FLOPs per RoI
(Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}) but not to their level. We have not isolated why
EMA-Det separates targets better.

\subsection{Hardware Feasibility Analysis}\label{sec:hardware}

A per-layer translation of the Block1$\to$Gate1$\to$Block4$\to$Gate2 path
into synthesizable C++ for high-level synthesis (HLS), with trained,
BatchNorm-folded weights validated against PyTorch, is in
\texttt{hardware/}. A static bit-width analysis
(\texttt{src/estimate\_hls\_bram.py}) identified on-chip memory as the main
constraint of the Zynq-7020 (630~KB; Block~1 alone used 30.3\%), which
motivated a 3-row line buffer (768~B) instead of a full-frame buffer. Block~1 was synthesized with Vitis Unified IDE 2025.2
(\texttt{xc7z020clg484-1}, 10~ns clock). C-simulation against the PyTorch
\texttt{Block1} reference gives a maximum absolute difference of
$3.3\times10^{-6}$ (\texttt{float}) and $1.48\times10^{-2}$
(\texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>}), consistent with input-quantization noise (the
accumulator uses \texttt{ap\_fixed<32,12>}). Unquantized \texttt{float} needs 784 DSP slices (356\% of the budget), 117{,}654
look-up tables (LUTs, 221\%), 73{,}950 flip-flops (FFs, 69\%) and 12 18-kb
block-RAM tiles (BRAM\_18K, 4\%) at a maximum clock frequency (Fmax) of
137.8~MHz, so it exceeds the DSP and LUT budgets by more than $3\times$ and
$2\times$. \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} at native operand width needs 145 DSPs
(65\%), 13{,}876 LUTs (26\%), 10{,}700 FFs (10\%) and no BRAM at 137.3~MHz:
$2.1\times$ fewer DSPs at equal accuracy, with $37\%$ DSP headroom.

C/register-transfer-level (RTL) co-simulation confirmed that the generated
RTL itself, not only HLS's C-simulation, reproduces this result:
\texttt{2/2} images passed at the same tolerance, with a deterministic
latency of 36{,}489 clock cycles ($\approx$365\,$\mu$s per image,
$\approx$2{,}740 images/s for this kernel alone). One layer is thus
verified numerically, by resource synthesis and by RTL co-simulation.

This covers only Block~1 (of six blocks plus two gates). It fully unrolls
every multiply--accumulate (MAC) operation (one multiplier per MAC,
initiation interval II$=1$), costing 145 DSPs for 144 MACs per pixel, which
is affordable only with one input channel. Block~2's second convolution
needs $9{,}216$ MACs per pixel ($\sim$42$\times$ the DSP budget under this
strategy) and Block~6's needs $589{,}824$ ($\sim$2{,}680$\times$). Blocks~2--6
therefore require a time-multiplexed MAC array, a systolic or
processing-element (PE) array reused over cycles. This array is not
designed yet: one layer is verified end to end, and the rest of the mapping
is quantified but unsolved. The roadmap follows from these figures.
(i)~\emph{Tiled PE array.} With $T_m$ output $\times$ $T_n$ input channels in
parallel and $T_mT_n\le 220$ DSPs, a layer takes MACs$/(T_mT_n)$
cycles~\cite{zhang2015fpga}; for the remaining $\approx$421M MACs (423.1M from
\texttt{thop} minus Block~1's 2.4M), all 220 DSPs at one MAC per cycle bound
the latency from below at $\approx$1.9M cycles ($\approx$14\,ms at 137\,MHz,
$\approx$72 images/s), before memory stalls. (ii)~\emph{Weight streaming.} The
1.53M parameters need $\approx$3.1\,MB at 16 bit ($\approx$1.5\,MB at 8 bit),
against 0.63\,MB of BRAM, so the weights of Blocks~2--6 must be streamed from
external double-data-rate (DDR) memory with double buffering, while
activations stay in line buffers as in Block~1; 8-bit quantization would halve
this traffic at an accuracy cost still to be measured. The depthwise variant
(Section~\ref{sec:cbam}) would fit on chip at 8 bit ($\approx$0.3\,MB) but
raises bandwidth pressure, and we have not synthesized it.
(iii)~\emph{Per-block validation.} Each block would repeat Block~1's chain of
C-simulation, synthesis and co-simulation.

\subsection{Cross-Sensor Generalization}\label{sec:crosssensor}

To test transfer to an unseen modality, we built an RoI verification
dataset from the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark~\cite{antiuav410}, using
its native video-level split (one video, \texttt{02\_6319}, appeared in both
partitions and was excluded). Positive patches were cropped around the
annotated drone box ($3\times$ context, $128\times128$); negatives combine
frames without a drone and hard negatives from the same frame, as in the EO
pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Sampling every 15th frame yields
27{,}768/12{,}388/16{,}808 train/val/test patches.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer.} The EO-trained final checkpoint (spatial bbox
head, no CBAM) was evaluated on the thermal val/test splits without
fine-tuning. Precision stays high (95.31\% val, 95.53\% test), but recall
falls from the EO in-domain $96.08\pm0.47\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:cbam}) to
86.29\% (val) and 80.12\% (test), and mAP@0.5 from 58.03\% to 52.05\% and
39.38\% (mAP@0.5:0.95: 21.82\% and 15.77\%). The learned features keep
their discriminative signal across modalities, but the difference between
EO and thermal silhouettes accounts for a sizeable share of the missed
detections.

\textbf{Thermal-from-scratch reference.} To bound the zero-shot result,
EMA-Det (final) was trained from scratch on the thermal train/val split under
the same protocol ($N{=}5$ seeds): mAP@0.5 $89.52\pm0.93\%$, mAP@0.5:0.95
$51.82\pm1.62\%$, recall $91.64\pm0.41\%$ and precision $97.55\pm0.39\%$.
mAP@0.5 and precision exceed both the zero-shot and the EO in-domain values;
recall exceeds the zero-shot values but stays below the EO in-domain
$96.08\pm0.47\%$. The same network (1{,}532{,}391 parameters, 423.085M FLOPs)
thus reaches 89.5\% mAP@0.5 on thermal data, which points to domain shift
rather than an architectural ceiling.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Dataset size.} Deduplication leaves 4{,}136 patches
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). \textbf{Cross-sensor generalization.} One
benchmark (Anti-UAV410); no domain adaptation or multi-sensor training.
\textbf{Hardware validation.} Only Block~1 is validated; the time-multiplexed
MAC array for Blocks~2--6 is unsolved (roadmap in Section~\ref{sec:hardware})
and no on-board latency or power is reported. \textbf{Early exit.} It costs
0.4--1.0 recall points (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}), and FPS is a GPU proxy.
\textbf{Initialization.} The Laplacian/Sobel prior shows no measurable gain
(Section~\ref{sec:cbam}). \textbf{Localization.} EMA-Det regresses one box from
a $4{\times}4$ map; its box recall and mAP@0.5:0.95 are well below those of
YOLOv8n, so it is a verifier, not a replacement detector. \textbf{Recall
definition.} Our recall is a classification-level acceptance rate that depends
on the score threshold (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}), so we also compare at a
fixed false-positive rate; all comparisons use one dataset.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This extended version reports EMA-Det under a controlled protocol with
$N{=}5$ seeds on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset. Decoupling the box
head from the pooled classification feature improves localization and explains
most of the earlier benefit of CBAM. Comparison with YOLOv8n and lightweight
backbones shows that mAP alone is an unreliable guide to verifier quality: in a
two-stage cascade, system-level recall is bounded by the verifier's acceptance
rate, not by its mAP. EMA-Det separates targets better than MobileNetV3-Small
and ShuffleNetV2 at higher computational cost, and than YOLOv8n at a fixed
false-alarm rate with far fewer FLOPs, but its localization is weaker. Early
exit saves 12.5--21.2\% of FLOPs for 0.4--1.0 recall points, and the
cross-sensor evaluation quantifies a real but not catastrophic EO$\to$thermal
gap. A synthesized first block and a quantified roadmap scope the work that
remains for a full FPGA mapping. We release the dataset-construction and
evaluation tools (\texttt{build\_dataset\_clean.py}, \texttt{check\_leakage.py},
\texttt{check\_raw\_duplicates.py}) so that similar datasets can be built
reproducibly from web-scraped imagery.
