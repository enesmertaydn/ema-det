\title[EMA-Det]{EMA-Det: Hardware-Aware Edge Verification for Two-Stage UAV Detection}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Enes Mert} \sur{Ayd{\i}n}}\email{23050211025@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Osman} \sur{Toklu{\u{g}}lu}}\email{otokluoglu@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Enver} \sur{{\c{C}}avu{\c{s}}}}\email{ecavus@aybu.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering}, \orgname{Ankara Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m Beyaz{\i}t University}, \orgaddress{\city{Ankara}, \country{T{\"u}rkiye}}}

\abstract{Autonomous detection of Low-Slow-Small (LSS) UAVs on edge devices typically relies on two-stage pipelines. In these systems, a lightweight proposal stage nominates candidate regions, which a verification network must subsequently evaluate under strict computational and memory constraints. This paper presents EMA-Det, a hardware-aware region-verification network designed to optimize this second stage. The architecture integrates a physically initialized (Laplacian/Sobel) first layer with two early-exit gates. These gates allow high-confidence background regions to bypass the majority of the network layers, effectively translating the highly asymmetric distribution of candidate regions into a measurable latency advantage. Evaluated on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset using a controlled $N{=}5$-seed, 150-epoch protocol, EMA-Det achieves a recall of $96.08\pm0.47\%$. In contrast, a full detection baseline (YOLOv8n) is limited to $73.75\pm1.86\%$ under identical conditions. This demonstrates that mean Average Precision (mAP) is a suboptimal primary optimization target for a second-stage verifier, since overall system-level recall $R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$ is bottlenecked by the weaker factor. To validate its viability for edge deployment, we present a static hardware-resource analysis alongside a synthesized, resource-verified FPGA implementation (Zynq-7020) of the network's first block. This substantiates the design's shift from FLOPs-oriented lightweight backbones toward a streaming, memory-bandwidth-aware architecture. Finally, cross-sensor evaluation on the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark shows the EO-trained verifier transfers zero-shot with precision intact, but recall falling to $80.12$--$86.29\%$ and mAP@0.5 to $39.38$--$52.05\%$. However, when the proposed EMA-Det architecture is trained from scratch on thermal data, it achieves an mAP@0.5 of $89.52\pm0.93\%$ and a recall of $91.64\pm0.41\%$. This confirms that the zero-shot gap reflects a genuine domain shift rather than an architectural ceiling.}

\keywords{LSS UAV Detection, Region Verification, Early-Exit, FPGA, Edge Computing, Cross-Sensor Generalization}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\textit{This manuscript extends a paper accepted at IEEE SIU
2026~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf} (proceedings pending), adding a
controlled, unified training protocol (Sections~\ref{sec:cbam}
and~\ref{sec:leakage}), a decoupled spatial bounding-box head
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}), and a cross-sensor generalization study
(Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}) absent from the conference version.}

Asymmetric threats posed by Low-Slow-Small (LSS) class Unmanned Aerial
Vehicles (UAVs) to civilian and military critical infrastructure require an
extremely fast and reliable ``sense-decide-act'' loop in modern autonomous
air-defense systems~\cite{luo2021threat,aydin2025aads}. EO sensors today
are typically 4K ($3840\times2160$) or higher; processing such
high-resolution streams directly and in full-frame on edge devices using
standard deep CNNs is infeasible in real time due to bandwidth and memory
constraints~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}. To alleviate this, fielded
autonomous systems typically use a two-stage
cascade~\cite{kiefer2023roi}: a first stage (radar
priors~\cite{nabati2019rrpn}, acoustic sensors~\cite{tegler2025sound}, or
motion/background-subtraction) identifies candidate RoIs, and a second
stage crops small patches and feeds them to a network for verification.

Prior work in this space commonly adapts general-purpose detectors (the
YOLO family, SSD) — originally developed for full-frame optical/aerial
object detection~\cite{li2020object} — to the RoI-verification
task~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. We argue, and demonstrate
empirically, that RoI verification in a two-stage system is \emph{not}
simply a scaled-down detection problem: on edge devices, memory bandwidth
is a more critical bottleneck than raw compute~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing};
even edge-compressed YOLO variants run into resource limits and
energy--performance bottlenecks on FPGA-class
hardware~\cite{adiono2021low,zhang2022apply}. Architectures designed for
full-frame operation, compressed into a micro-input regime, suffer
spatial feature loss from their deep global-pooling stages and asymmetric
data flow. Since total system recall is $R_{\text{total}} =
R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, a verifier's false
negative is a system-level miss, making recall — not mAP — the
design-critical metric for the second stage.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) We formalize the RoI-verification problem
and show that full-frame attention carries a training-stability cost in
the micro-input regime rather than a uniform accuracy penalty
(Section~\ref{sec:cbam}). (2) We convert the asymmetric background
distribution of candidate regions into a latency advantage via an
early-exit mechanism adapted to this setting (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
(3) We present a source-level-split, deduplicated RoI
dataset-construction methodology enforcing a strict train/test boundary,
released for reuse (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). (4) We report a
controlled, $N{=}5$-seed comparison under one unified training protocol
across all ablations and baselines. (5) We show that decoupling the
bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) improves localization and accounts for
most of CBAM's previously observed benefit. (6) We measure cross-sensor
(EO$\to$thermal-IR) generalization on Anti-UAV410
(Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}), zero-shot and via a
thermal-from-scratch reference, quantifying the domain-shift gap.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Two-stage and cascade detection.} Two-stage detectors separate
proposal from verification, trading latency for accuracy relative to
single-stage designs~\cite{ren2015faster}; cascade architectures refine
localization through successive, increasing-IoU stages~\cite{cai2018cascade}.
UAV-specific work follows the same system-level split, pairing a
lightweight proposal stage with a separate verification
network~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. EMA-Det targets the
verification stage specifically, formalized via $R_{\text{total}} =
R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:intro})
rather than treating it as a scaled-down general detector.

\paragraph{Efficient edge backbones.} Mobile-oriented backbones cut FLOPs
mainly via depthwise-separable or grouped
convolutions~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} and channel-shuffle
operations~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}, both optimized against mobile-CPU cost
models. FPGA accelerator work shows these operations instead raise
memory-bandwidth pressure on BRAM-constrained
fabrics~\cite{zhao2024fpga} — consistent with EMA-Det's choice of standard
convolutions in a shallow, streaming-friendly backbone
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\paragraph{Early-exit inference.} Confidence-gated early exits let easy
inputs skip later stages~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,liang2024implementation},
surveyed broadly in~\cite{rahmath2024earlyexit}. Prior work targets
general classification with no strong class prior; EMA-Det's gates
(Section~\ref{sec:gates}) instead exploit the RoI-verification setting's
specific background-majority asymmetry.

\paragraph{Attention and small-object features.} Channel-spatial attention
(CBAM) recalibrates features at negligible cost~\cite{woo2018cbam};
separately, stride-free downsampling has been proposed to preserve
small-object spatial information lost to strided
pooling~\cite{sunkara2022spdconv}. Sections~\ref{sec:cbam}
and~\ref{sec:spatialbbox} reach a related conclusion for the micro-input
setting via a decoupled spatial bbox head rather than attention alone.

\paragraph{Cross-sensor UAV detection.} Cross-modality UAV detection has
been studied as EO/IR fusion~\cite{sun2022dronevehicle} and, for the
benchmark used here, as single-sensor thermal-IR
detection~\cite{antiuav410}. This paper does not fuse modalities;
Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor} instead measures the zero-shot
EO$\to$thermal generalization gap directly.

\section{EMA-Det: Architecture and Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System-Level Latency Model}\label{sec:latency}
In a two-stage architecture, total system latency $T_{\text{total}}$ is
modeled as the proposal time plus the sum of per-candidate verification
times (Eq.~\ref{eq:latency}).
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{proposal}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RoI}}} T_{\text{verifier}}^{(i)}
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
When a general-purpose full-frame detector is used as the verifier, every
candidate patch — including empty-sky patches — pays the full network's
fixed cost. EMA-Det's dynamic early-exit structure converts this linear
scaling into an asymptotic one.

\subsection{Laplacian/Sobel-Initialized Kernel Bank}\label{sec:init}
At a constrained $128\times128$ resolution, small targets carry limited
spatial information. Standard Xavier/Kaiming random initialization requires
the network to learn edge boundaries from scratch, increasing iteration cost
and training variance. The first convolution layer's weights $W_1$ are
instead initialized from a deterministic bank of Laplacian ($L$)
\cite{marr1980theory} and Sobel ($S$) \cite{kanopoulos1988design} operators:
\begin{equation}
W^{(0)}_{1,c} = \begin{cases}
K_c, & c \in \{1,\dots,K\} \\
R_c, & c \in \{K+1,\dots,C_{\text{out}}\}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
where $K_c$ are the deterministic physical priors ($L$, $L_2$, $S_x$, $S_y$,
and their signed sums) and $R_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ preserves the
layer's capacity to generalize beyond the fixed operators. These weights
are left unfrozen and are updated during training like any other layer.
The rationale follows the general inductive-bias argument for physically
motivated initialization: seeding the first layer with known
edge-detection operators provides a useful prior over local gradient
structure, rather than requiring it be discovered from random weights —
expected to matter particularly for small targets at $128\times128$
resolution, where edge information is a large fraction of the available
signal. This is presented as motivation for the design choice rather than
an empirically isolated effect, since a controlled convergence-curve
comparison against random initialization under matched seeds and budget
is not reported here and is identified as future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Block~1 (Section~\ref{sec:init})
reduces $1\times128\times128$ to $16\times64\times64$; Blocks~2--4
progressively reduce to $128\times8\times8$; Block~5 fuses two parallel
dilated branches ($d{=}1,2$) to $128\times4\times4$; Block~6 expands to
$256\times4\times4$; a verification head (global average pool, dropout
$0.4$) produces a classification logit, and a separate spatial branch
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) produces the 4-coordinate bounding-box
refinement.

CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} was evaluated during architecture development and is
\emph{not} part of the default forward path — Section~\ref{sec:cbam}
presents controlled evidence for that design decision.

\subsubsection{Spatial Bounding-Box Head}\label{sec:spatialbbox}
Retaining spatial feature information for box regression, rather than
regressing from a globally pooled classification vector, is standard
practice in modern detection architectures. An earlier internal
configuration pooled before both branches, discarding spatial layout
prior to box regression. This is corrected by decoupling the two heads:
classification remains on the pooled vector, while the box head reads the
pre-pooling $256\times4\times4$ feature map directly (flattened into a
single linear layer). Table~\ref{tab:spatialbbox} reports a controlled
$N{=}5$ ablation: the spatial head improves mAP@0.75 (+5.7 points) and
mAP@0.5:0.95 (+2.9 points) at a cost of only 15K additional parameters
(${\sim}1\%$) and negligible FLOPs, while recall is essentially unchanged
and its cross-seed std decreases ($2.54\to0.47$). It is the default
throughout this paper; the pooled-vector configuration remains available
(\texttt{use\_spatial\_bbox=False} in \texttt{ema\_det.py}).

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}
The verification head is optimized with a combined classification and
box-regression loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, p) + \lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}(\hat{b}, b)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}$ is the Complete-IoU loss~\cite{zheng2020distance}.
In this study, $\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$ and $\lambda_{\text{box}}=2.0$.
Early-exit gates are supervised with their own BCE terms
($\lambda_{\text{gate1}}=0.3$, $\lambda_{\text{gate2}}=0.4$) against the
same binary label as the main head.

\subsection{Dynamic Early-Exit Gates}\label{sec:gates}
Following the confidence-gated early-exit paradigm
(e.g.\ BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}), Gates~1 and~2
(after Blocks~2 and~4) implement the asymptotic rule of
Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}: a candidate halts (classified background, no box
refinement) once its confidence falls below $1-\tau$; otherwise it proceeds.
\begin{equation}
\text{Result} = \begin{cases}
\text{Background (halt)}, & \hat{y}_i \le (1-\tau) \\
C_{i+1}(F_i)\ (\text{continue}), & \hat{y}_i > (1-\tau)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}\label{sec:leakage}

The dataset is built from two public raw sources: rotary-wing and
fixed-wing LSS UAV photographs from a Kaggle
collection~\cite{dasmehdixtr_kaggle} (labeled, 80\% of the raw pool), and
bird/empty-sky photographs from a Roboflow Universe
collection~\cite{mohsen_roboflow} (unlabeled, hard-negative source, 20\%).
The combined raw pool comprises 8{,}793 source photographs (5{,}921
labeled UAV candidates, 2{,}872 sky/negative images).

Both source collections are web-aggregated and include sequentially
extracted video frames, so a substantial fraction of raw photographs are
near-duplicates. To guarantee an uncontaminated evaluation, an
$8\times8$ average-hash comparison (Hamming distance $\le 5$, clustered
via union-find, one representative retained per cluster, preferring a
labeled member) is applied to the raw pool prior to patch extraction,
reducing it to 2{,}142 unique labeled-candidate and 887 unique sky
photographs. This deduplicated pool is split into train/val/test
(70/15/15) \emph{before} patch cropping, so every patch inherits its
source photograph's split, precluding source-level overlap. One positive
patch ($3\times$ context factor) and one hard-negative patch (same
photograph, near but not overlapping the UAV) are cropped per photograph.

This protocol is deliberately conservative, prioritizing a strict
evaluation boundary over raw dataset size. The resulting corpus comprises
4{,}136 patches (2{,}842/662/632 train/val/test), each traceable to a
unique source photograph with no cross-split duplication. All results
here use this dataset; values in the conference
version~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf}, which used a patch-level split without
source-level deduplication, are superseded and should not be compared
directly.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All models are trained with AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), 5-epoch
linear warmup, cosine annealing, batch size 16, and gradient clipping
(norm 5.0), with early stopping (patience 20 on val.\ mAP@0.5, up to 150
epochs). All results, including the CBAM ablation, use $N{=}5$ seeds
$\{42, 7, 123, 2024, 31415\}$ under this identical protocol. Augmentation
excludes Mosaic/MixUp (destroys small-target shape at $128\times128$);
we use horizontal flip, brightness/contrast jitter, motion blur,
Gaussian noise, small-angle rotation, and coarse dropout.

With the spatial bbox head in place, mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75, and mAP@0.5:0.95

remaining cost is recall: $3$ points lower on average
($93.23\pm1.37\%$ vs.\ $96.08\pm0.47\%$) and less stable across seeds

this basis.

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} supports the central claim of
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} — a full detection
architecture with a multi-scale head and DFL-based box regression —
reaches substantially higher mAP@0.5:0.95 than EMA-Det
($49.59\pm0.45\%$ vs.\ $28.04\pm1.90\%$, ${\sim}1.8\times$), reflecting
its localization advantage even after the spatial bbox head
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) narrows part of the gap. However,
YOLOv8n's recall is capped at $73.75\pm1.86\%$ (tight cross-seed
variance, indicating an architectural property rather than seed noise),
well below every other model in the table. Given $R_{\text{total}} =
R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, this recall deficit
illustrates the failure mode this paper argues against: a verifier
optimized for, and evaluated by, mAP can appear stronger on the commonly
reported metric while functioning as a weaker second-stage verifier
under the metric that determines system-level performance. The two
lightweight backbones (MobileNetV3-Small~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3},
ShuffleNetV2 x0.5~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}), trained from scratch with no
architectural changes beyond a 1-channel first layer and the same head
as EMA-Det, achieve comparable mAP/recall despite 40--77\% fewer
parameters and lower nominal FLOPs; they are competitive generic
backbones but lack EMA-Det's early-exit savings (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit})
or its recall stability advantage over CBAM (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}).

This FLOPs disparity motivates, rather than undermines, EMA-Det's use of
standard convolutions in place of depthwise-separable designs: FLOPs
proxy arithmetic cost rather than hardware efficiency, and
depthwise-separable convolutions are documented to raise
memory-bandwidth pressure on BRAM-constrained FPGA fabrics despite their
lower operation count (Section~\ref{sec:related})~\cite{zhao2024fpga,qasaimeh2019comparing}.
This study does not synthesize MobileNetV3-Small or ShuffleNetV2 to FPGA
hardware, so Section~\ref{sec:hardware}'s resource-utilization comparison
is restricted to EMA-Det's own Block~1.

\subsection{Hardware Feasibility Analysis}\label{sec:hardware}

This section reports measured results together with a static resource
analysis scoping the network's FPGA mapping. Both configurations were
exported to ONNX and verified against PyTorch (max.\ absolute difference
$1.7\times10^{-5}$/$1.3\times10^{-5}$, 20 random inputs). At batch size 1
(matching single-RoI streaming deployment), PyTorch-CUDA reaches
378.7/248.8 FPS (final/CBAM) while ONNX Runtime's CPU provider reaches
738.1/646.5 FPS — higher than GPU, since GPU gains come from batch
parallelism and a sequential, early-exit-gated single-RoI workload
(Table~\ref{tab:earlyexit}) does not present a large-batch load. The
CPU/ONNX and FPGA figures below are therefore the representative
reference points for edge deployment. The spatial bbox head costs some
throughput over the legacy head (465.0/262.6 FPS, same setting), reported
alongside its accuracy gain (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}).

A per-layer translation of the Block1$\to$Gate1$\to$Block4$\to$Gate2 path
into synthesizable C++ (trained, BatchNorm-folded weights, validated
against PyTorch) is provided in \texttt{hardware/}. A static bit-width
analysis (\texttt{src/estimate\_hls\_bram.py}) of this fully-buffered
translation identified on-chip memory as the primary constraint for the
Zynq-7020 (630~KB budget), Block~1 alone accounting for 30.3\% of the
total — directly informing the streaming redesign below.

Block~1 was restructured around a 3-row line buffer (768~B, replacing
the full-frame buffer) and synthesized with Vitis Unified IDE 2025.2
(Zynq-7020, \texttt{xc7z020clg484-1}, 10~ns clock target). C-simulation
against the PyTorch \texttt{Block1} reference confirms numerical
correctness in both representations: max.\ absolute difference
$3.3\times10^{-6}$ (\texttt{float}) and $1.48\times10^{-2}$
(\texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>}), consistent with input-activation quantization
noise (accumulator uses the wider \texttt{ap\_fixed<32,12>}).
Table~\ref{tab:block1synth} reports both representations: unquantized
\texttt{float} fits the BRAM budget but overshoots DSP/LUT by more than
$3\times$/$2\times$ — floating-point is far more expensive in hardware
than fixed-point. Quantizing to \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} and multiplying
at native width rather than pre-widened operands ($2.1\times$ fewer
DSPs, equal accuracy) brings every resource under budget, with $37\%$
DSP headroom.

C/RTL co-simulation subsequently confirmed that the generated RTL itself
(not merely HLS's C-simulation of the pre-synthesis source) reproduces
this result: \texttt{2/2} images passed at the same tolerance, with a
measured, deterministic latency of 36{,}489 clock cycles
($\approx$365\,$\mu$s/image, $\approx$2{,}740 images/s for this kernel
alone). This is a real, tool-verified result for one layer — confirmed
numerically, by resource synthesis, and by RTL co-simulation.

This result covers only Block~1 (of six blocks plus two gates) and does
not simply repeat for the rest of the network: Block~1 fully unrolls
every MAC (one multiplier per MAC, II$=1$), costing 145 DSPs for 144
MACs/pixel — affordable only with one input channel. MACs/pixel scale
with channel depth: Block~2's second conv needs $9{,}216$ MACs/pixel
($\sim$42$\times$ the chip's DSP budget under the same strategy), and
Block~6's needs $589{,}824$ ($\sim$2{,}680$\times$). Block~2 onward
therefore require a time-multiplexed MAC array (a systolic/PE-array
design reused over cycles) rather than full unrolling — not yet designed
past Block~1. This is reported transparently: one layer is fully
verified end-to-end; the rest of the network's hardware mapping is
quantified but unsolved.

\subsection{Cross-Sensor Generalization}\label{sec:crosssensor}

To assess EO-domain transfer to an unseen modality, an RoI verification
dataset was constructed from the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR
benchmark~\cite{antiuav410}, using its native video-level train/val/test
split (one video, \texttt{02\_6319}, spanned both partitions and was
excluded as a precaution). Positive patches were cropped around the
annotated drone box ($3\times$ context, resized to $128\times128$);
negatives combine drone-absent frames with same-frame hard negatives,
mirroring the EO pipeline's negative-mining strategy
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Every 15th frame was sampled to limit
near-duplicates, yielding 27{,}768/12{,}388/16{,}808 train/val/test
patches.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer.} The EO-trained final checkpoint
(spatial-bbox, no CBAM) was evaluated directly on the thermal val/test
splits without fine-tuning (Table~\ref{tab:crosssensor}). Precision stays
high (95.3--95.5\%), while recall falls from the EO in-domain
$96.08\pm0.47\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:cbam}) to 80--86\%, and mAP@0.5 falls to
39--52\%. This indicates the verifier's Laplacian/Sobel-initialized first
layer and learned features retain discriminative UAV/non-UAV signal
across modalities, while EO/thermal-IR silhouette shift accounts for a
non-trivial fraction of missed detections.

\textbf{Thermal-from-scratch reference.} To bound the zero-shot result,
EMA-Det (final configuration) was trained from scratch on the thermal
train/val split under the identical protocol used throughout this paper.
Table~\ref{tab:crosssensor}'s third row reports the result: mAP@0.5
$89.52\pm0.93\%$, recall $91.64\pm0.41\%$, precision $97.55\pm0.39\%$, all
above both the zero-shot and EO in-domain values. This establishes that
the zero-shot gap reflects genuine domain shift rather than an
architectural ceiling: the same network (1{,}532{,}391 params, 423.085M
FLOPs) reaches 89.5\% mAP@0.5 trained directly on thermal data. The higher
mAP/precision on thermal data are consistent with thermal-IR UAV
signatures presenting as high-contrast, low-clutter targets relative to
EO imagery.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Dataset size:} the deduplication protocol (Section~\ref{sec:leakage})
yields 4{,}136 patches from a raw pool of 8{,}793 photographs, trading
sample count for evaluation rigor; expanding the raw pool is future work.
\textbf{Cross-sensor generalization:} Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}
reports zero-shot and from-scratch results on a single benchmark
(Anti-UAV410); domain adaptation and multi-sensor joint training were not
evaluated. \textbf{Hardware validation:} Section~\ref{sec:hardware}
synthesizes and cosimulation-verifies Block~1 only; the time-multiplexed
MAC array for Blocks~2--6 remains future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This extended version reports EMA-Det under a controlled, $N{=}5$-seed
protocol on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset. Decoupling the
bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature improves
localization and accounts for most of CBAM's previously observed benefit
— indicating that benefit substituted for discarded spatial information
rather than an independent localization gain. Comparison against a full
detection architecture (YOLOv8n) and lightweight backbones establishes
that mAP alone is an unreliable indicator of verifier quality, since
recall — not mAP — determines system-level performance under a
two-stage cascade. Cross-sensor evaluation on Anti-UAV410 quantifies a
genuine but non-catastrophic EO$\to$thermal domain-shift gap, and a
hardware feasibility analysis — including a synthesized,
resource-verified implementation of the network's first block — scopes
the work remaining for a full FPGA mapping. We release the
dataset-construction and evaluation tooling
(\texttt{build\_dataset\_clean.py}, \texttt{check\_leakage.py},
\texttt{check\_raw\_duplicates.py}) to support reproducible construction
of similar datasets from web-aggregated source imagery.
