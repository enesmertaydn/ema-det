\title[EMA-Det]{EMA-Det: Hardware-Aware Edge Verification for Two-Stage UAV Detection}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Enes Mert} \sur{Ayd{\i}n}}\email{23050211025@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Osman} \sur{Tokluo{\u{g}}lu}}\email{otokluoglu@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Enver} \sur{{\c{C}}avu{\c{s}}}}\email{ecavus@aybu.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering}, \orgname{Ankara Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m Beyaz{\i}t University}, \orgaddress{\city{Ankara}, \country{T{\"u}rkiye}}}

\abstract{Detecting Low-Slow-Small (LSS) unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) on edge devices usually takes two stages: a lightweight proposal stage nominates candidate regions, and a verification network checks them under tight compute and memory budgets. This paper presents EMA-Det, a hardware-aware verification network for that second stage. It combines a first layer initialized from Laplacian/Sobel kernels, two early-exit gates that let confident background regions skip most of the network, and a centre-based localization head whose score reflects box quality. On a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset, under a controlled protocol ($N{=}5$ seeds, 150 epochs), EMA-Det reaches $96.46\pm0.81\%$ verification recall (target accepted, box not checked), above MobileNetV3-Small ($94.05\pm1.39\%$) and ShuffleNetV2 ($93.80\pm1.11\%$) trained identically. At a $5\%$ false-positive rate it accepts $94.8\pm1.9\%$ of the targets, against $88.4\pm2.8\%$ for a COCO-pretrained and $79.5\pm3.1\%$ for a from-scratch YOLOv8n, a full-frame detector with 17 times the FLOPs. As a detector it is competitive but weaker (mAP@0.5 $73.6\%$ against $79.5\%$ and $84.0\%$; box recall $77.6\%$). Mean average precision (mAP) alone is an unreliable guide to verifier quality: system-level recall, $R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, is bounded by the weaker factor. We also give a static hardware-resource analysis and a synthesized, resource-verified field-programmable gate array (FPGA) implementation (Zynq-7020) of the first block. On the Anti-UAV410 thermal-infrared (IR) benchmark, zero-shot transfer from electro-optical (EO) images keeps precision but drops recall to $79.57$--$86.53\%$; trained from scratch on thermal data, EMA-Det reaches mAP@0.5 of $90.73\pm0.42\%$ and recall of $91.89\pm0.29\%$, which points to domain shift rather than an architectural ceiling.}

\keywords{LSS UAV Detection, Region Verification, Early-Exit, FPGA, Edge Computing, Cross-Sensor Generalization}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\textit{This manuscript extends a paper published at IEEE SIU
2026~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf} with a controlled unified protocol
(Sections~\ref{sec:cbam} and~\ref{sec:leakage}), a centre-based
localization head (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) and a
cross-sensor study (Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}).}

\looseness=-1 Low-Slow-Small (LSS) unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) pose an asymmetric
threat to civilian and military infrastructure and call for a fast,
reliable sense--decide--act loop~\cite{luo2021threat,aydin2025aads}.
Electro-optical (EO) sensors now typically deliver 4K ($3840\times2160$)
or more, which standard deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) cannot
process at full frame in real time on edge devices, because of bandwidth
and memory limits~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}. Fielded systems therefore
use a two-stage cascade~\cite{kiefer2023roi}: a first stage (radar~\cite{nabati2019rrpn},
acoustic~\cite{tegler2025sound} or motion cues) proposes candidate regions of interest
(RoIs), and a second stage crops small patches and verifies them.

\looseness=-1 Prior work usually adapts general-purpose detectors (the YOLO family,
SSD)~\cite{li2020object} to RoI
verification~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. We argue that RoI
verification is \emph{not} just a scaled-down detection problem. On edge
devices memory bandwidth is a tighter bottleneck than raw
compute~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}, and compressed YOLO variants run into
resource and energy--performance limits on field-programmable gate array
(FPGA)-class hardware~\cite{adiono2021low,zhang2022apply}. Full-frame
architectures squeezed into a micro-input regime also lose spatial
information through deep global pooling. And because total system recall is
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, every
false negative of the verifier is a miss for the whole system. Recall, more
than mAP, is therefore the critical metric for the second stage (though not
the only thing that decides system performance).

\paragraph{What ``hardware-aware'' means here.} Design choices follow the
constraints of the target FPGA (on-chip block RAM (BRAM), digital signal
processing (DSP) slices and memory bandwidth), not just floating-point
operations (FLOPs): (i)~standard instead of depthwise-separable convolutions,
which put more pressure on bandwidth in BRAM-limited fabrics; (ii)~a
streaming-friendly backbone whose first block needs only a 3-row line buffer;
(iii)~per-RoI early-exit gates, which suit a verifier that handles one RoI at
a time; and (iv)~fixed-point arithmetic checked with high-level synthesis.
Only (ii) for Block~1 and (iv) are verified in hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:hardware}); (i) and (iii) are design rationale.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1)~\emph{Recall-centred verifier design.} We treat RoI verification as the
second factor of $R_{\text{total}}$. Over $N{=}5$ seeds, a small purpose-built
network exceeds the verification recall of identically trained lightweight
backbones by about 2 points and, at a fixed false-alarm rate, that of YOLOv8n
by 6 to 15 points, with 17 times fewer FLOPs; its localization (mAP@0.5 of
73.6\% against 79.5\% for a from-scratch YOLOv8n) is competitive but weaker
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).
(2)~\emph{Background-aware early exit.} Two confidence gates let
background candidates skip most of the network, cutting average FLOPs per
RoI by 13.9--21.9\% at a cost of 0.3--0.5 recall points
(Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}). (3)~\emph{A localization head with a quality-aware score.} A box head that
keeps spatial information explains most of the benefit that the Convolutional
Block Attention Module (CBAM)~\cite{woo2018cbam} appeared to give (CBAM costs
recall on top of it), and a centre-based head whose score is the classification
probability times the heat-map peak raises mAP@0.5 from 58.0 to 73.6\%,
confirmed on independent thermal data (Sections~\ref{sec:spatialbbox} and~\ref{sec:cbam}).
(4)~\emph{Hardware feasibility.} Block~1 is synthesized and co-simulated on a
Zynq-7020, and the scaling limits and a roadmap for the remaining blocks are
quantified (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}). We also release a deduplicated,
source-level-split dataset pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) and measure
EO$\to$thermal-IR transfer on Anti-UAV410 (Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\looseness=-1 \paragraph{Two-stage detection and efficient backbones.} Two-stage
detectors separate proposal from verification and trade latency for
accuracy relative to single-stage designs~\cite{ren2015faster}; cascades
refine localization over stages with increasing intersection-over-union
(IoU) thresholds~\cite{cai2018cascade}. EMA-Det instead targets
the verification stage itself. Mobile backbones cut FLOPs with
depthwise-separable or grouped convolutions~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3}
and channel shuffle~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}, but FPGA studies show that
these raise memory-bandwidth pressure on BRAM-constrained
fabrics~\cite{zhao2024fpga}. That is consistent with EMA-Det's shallow
backbone of standard convolutions (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}); FPGA accelerator
design is set by the balance of compute and memory bandwidth~\cite{zhang2015fpga}.

\looseness=-1 \paragraph{UAV and small-object detection.} Anti-UAV
detection and tracking has been widely
benchmarked~\cite{zhao2022antiuav,jiang2021antiuav,zhu2021visdrone};
Seidaliyeva et al.~\cite{seidaliyeva2020drone} detect drones from motion cues and a
CNN classifier, and small objects are tackled, e.g., by sliced
inference~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. These works mostly evaluate full-frame detection by
mAP, whereas EMA-Det verifies candidates and selects models by recall.
Cross-modality UAV detection has been studied as EO/IR
fusion~\cite{sun2022dronevehicle} and single-sensor thermal-IR
detection~\cite{antiuav410}; Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor} quantifies our verifier's
zero-shot EO$\to$thermal gap.

\looseness=-1 \paragraph{Early-exit inference and attention.} Confidence-gated early exits let
easy inputs skip later stages~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,liang2024implementation}
(surveyed in~\cite{rahmath2024earlyexit}), usually for classification without a
strong class prior; EMA-Det's gates (Section~\ref{sec:gates}) instead exploit the
background majority of RoI verification. The Convolutional Block Attention Module
(CBAM) recalibrates channel and spatial features at negligible
cost~\cite{woo2018cbam}; Sections~\ref{sec:cbam} and~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}
reach a related conclusion with a spatial localization head instead of attention.

\section{EMA-Det: Architecture and Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System-Level Latency Model}\label{sec:latency}
In a two-stage system, total latency is the proposal time plus the
verification times of all candidates,
$T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{proposal}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RoI}}} T_{\text{verifier}}^{(i)}$.
A full-frame detector makes every patch, even empty sky, pay the full cost of
the network. EMA-Det's early exits keep the linear dependence on
$N_{\text{RoI}}$ but lower the average cost per candidate $\bar T_{\text{verifier}}$:
$T_{\text{total}}\approx T_{\text{proposal}}+N_{\text{RoI}}\,\bar T_{\text{verifier}}$.

\subsection{Laplacian/Sobel-Initialized Kernel Bank}\label{sec:init}
At $128\times128$ resolution, small targets carry little spatial
information. We initialize the weights $W_1$ of
the first convolution layer from a deterministic bank of Laplacian
($L$)~\cite{marr1980theory} and Sobel ($S$)~\cite{kanopoulos1988design}
operators:
\begin{equation}
W^{(0)}_{1,c} = \begin{cases}
K_c, & c \in \{1,\dots,K\} \\
R_c, & c \in \{K+1,\dots,C_{\text{out}}\}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
Here $K_c$ are the deterministic physical priors ($L$, $L_2$, $S_x$,
$S_y$ and their signed sums), in the spirit of hand-designed filter priors
in CNNs~\cite{luan2018gabor}, and $R_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ is a
random term, as in standard random initialization~\cite{he2015delving},
which keeps the layer free to learn beyond the fixed operators. The
weights are not frozen; they are updated like those of any other layer.
The motivation is an inductive-bias argument (a prior on local gradient
structure), but Section~\ref{sec:cbam} finds no measurable gain over random
initialization. Figure~\ref{fig:featmaps} shows the Block~1 responses: the
fixed channels give clean edge maps of a UAV and of a bird, and the trained
kernels stay close to their priors (cosine similarity $0.9998$ on average,
minimum $0.9968$). A random layer also responds to edges (last column), so the
figure illustrates the prior without proving a benefit.

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Block~1 (Section~\ref{sec:init})
turns $1\times128\times128$ into $16\times64\times64$; Blocks~2--4 reduce
this step by step to $128\times8\times8$; Block~5 fuses two parallel
dilated branches ($d{=}1,2$) into $128\times4\times4$; Block~6 expands it
to $256\times4\times4$. A verification head (global average pooling (GAP),
dropout $0.4$) produces a classification logit, and a centre-based
localization head (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) fuses the Block~3, 4 and 6
maps into a $16{\times}16$ grid of target-centre scores, offsets and sizes. CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} is \emph{not} part of the default forward
path (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}).

\subsection{Localization Head}\label{sec:spatialbbox}
Keeping spatial feature information for box regression, instead of regressing
from a globally pooled vector, is standard practice in modern
detectors~\cite{ren2015faster,liu2016ssd}. We compare three heads
(Table~\ref{tab:cbam}). The \emph{pooled-vector} head of an earlier
configuration discards spatial layout. A \emph{spatial regression} head reads
the $256\times4\times4$ map before pooling (one linear layer); this improves
mAP@0.75 by 5.7 points and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 2.9 points for 15K extra parameters,
with a smaller spread across seeds ($2.54\to0.47$ in recall), but it scores a box
by the classification probability alone, which ignores localization quality. Our
final \emph{centre-based} head (CenterNet-style~\cite{zhou2019objects}) predicts,
on a $16{\times}16$ grid fused from the Block~3, 4 and 6 maps, a heat map of the
target centre and, per cell, an offset and a size. The box is that of the peak cell
and the detection score is the classification probability times the peak value,
so it reflects localization quality. It adds 0.12M parameters and 60M FLOPs to the
regression head.

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}
The verification head combines a classification term and a localization term
(Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, p) + \lambda_{\text{loc}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$ is the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss and
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}$ is a CenterNet focal loss~\cite{zhou2019objects} on the heat
map (all-zero target for background patches) plus, on the $3{\times}3$ cells around
the centre cell, the Complete-IoU (CIoU) loss~\cite{zheng2020distance} and an L1 loss
on offset and size; the regression head of Table~\ref{tab:cbam} uses CIoU alone.
$\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$ and $\lambda_{\text{loc}}=2.0$ (applied to the box terms; the
heat-map term has weight 1). The early-exit gates add their own BCE terms
($\lambda_{\text{gate1}}=0.3$, $\lambda_{\text{gate2}}=0.4$) against the same binary label.

\subsection{Dynamic Early-Exit Gates}\label{sec:gates}
Following the confidence-gated early-exit idea
(e.g.\ BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}), Gates~1 and~2
(after Blocks~2 and~4) apply the threshold rule of Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}: a
candidate halts, classified as background with no box refinement, once
its confidence falls below $1-\tau$; otherwise it continues.
\begin{equation}
\text{Result} = \begin{cases}
\text{Background (halt)}, & \hat{y}_i \le (1-\tau) \\
C_{i+1}(F_i)\ (\text{continue}), & \hat{y}_i > (1-\tau)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}\label{sec:leakage}

\looseness=-1 The dataset comes from two public raw sources: photographs of rotary- and
fixed-wing LSS UAVs from a Kaggle collection~\cite{dasmehdixtr_kaggle}
(labeled, 80\% of the raw pool) and bird and empty-sky photographs from a
Roboflow Universe collection~\cite{mohsen_roboflow} (unlabeled hard
negatives, 20\%). Together they contain 8{,}793 photographs: 5{,}921
labeled UAV candidates and 2{,}872 negatives. Both collections were
scraped from the web and include frames extracted in sequence from videos,
so many photographs are near-duplicates. To keep the evaluation
uncontaminated, we compare $8\times8$ average hashes (Hamming distance
$\le 5$), cluster them with union-find and keep one representative per
cluster, preferring a labeled one, before patch extraction. This leaves 2{,}142
unique labeled and 887 unique sky photographs, split into train/val/test
(70\%/15\%/15\%) \emph{before} cropping, so every patch inherits the split of
its source photograph. From each photograph we crop one positive patch
($3\times$ context factor) and one hard-negative patch (same photograph, near
but not overlapping the UAV), giving 4{,}136 patches (2{,}842/662/632
train/val/test). Values from the conference version~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf}
came from a patch-level split without deduplication and are superseded.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All models are trained with AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), a 5-epoch
linear warmup, cosine annealing, batch size 16 and gradient clipping (norm
5.0), with early stopping (patience 20 on validation mAP@0.5, at most 150
epochs). All results, including the CBAM ablation, use $N{=}5$ seeds
$\{42, 7, 123, 2024, 31415\}$ under this same protocol. Augmentation uses
flips, brightness/contrast jitter, motion blur, noise, small rotations and
coarse dropout.

\subsection{CBAM Trade-off and Initialization Ablations}\label{sec:cbam}

\looseness=-1 Table~\ref{tab:cbam} reports the controlled $N{=}5$ comparison. Rows
above the line use the spatial regression head
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}); below the line are the final centre-based head and two variants.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Localization head.} The centre-based head changes localization only:
mAP@0.5 rises from $58.03$ to $73.58\%$ ($p=0.0001$), mAP@0.5:0.95 from $28.04$ to
$40.60\%$ ($p=0.005$) and box recall from $71.7$ to $77.6\%$ ($p=0.003$), with recall
and AUROC unchanged ($p=0.53$, $0.40$); strict-IoU metrics vary more across seeds
(mAP@0.5:0.95 from 32.9 to 44.8\%). The head was designed after an error
analysis on the EO test split, which showed that the loss comes from box quality and
from scores that ignore it; the same analysis on the validation split gives the same
picture, and two sets outside it agree: the EO validation split (mAP@0.5 $+11.2$ points,
$p=0.001$) and the Anti-UAV410 thermal data (five seeds from scratch: mAP@0.5:0.95
$51.82\pm1.62\to56.59\pm0.90\%$, $p=0.003$; mAP@0.75 $53.4\to61.8\%$, $p=0.006$; mAP@0.5
$89.5\to90.7\%$, $p=0.14$, near its ceiling). On an external visible-light set unused in training or selection (DUT Anti-UAV test~\cite{zhao2022antiuav}, $2{,}043+2{,}043$ patches, zero-shot, five seeds), recall is $94.5\pm1.2\%$, recall at $5\%$ FPR $90.4\pm1.6\%$ and AUROC $0.980$, while mAP@0.5 falls to $55.7\pm5.2\%$ under domain shift.

\looseness=-1 With the regression head, a paired $t$-test over the five matched seeds
finds no significant difference between configurations in mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75
or mAP@0.5:0.95 ($p=0.89$, $0.77$, $0.83$). CBAM still costs recall:
$93.23\pm1.37\%$ against $96.08\pm0.47\%$ (paired $t$-test, $p=0.017$). With
the legacy pooled-vector head, 2 of 5 CBAM seeds collapsed to 74--79\%
recall; this is absent with the spatial head (worst run 90.8\%). CBAM thus seems
to have made up for spatial information that pooling had discarded; once that is
restored it adds no localization benefit, so the final model leaves it out.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Initialization.} The two initialization rows of Table~\ref{tab:cbam}
repeat the regression-head model with a random first layer and with the twelve fixed kernels
frozen (same seeds and protocol). The differences are within noise (paired
$t$-test, random initialization: mAP@0.5 $p=0.98$, recall $p=0.93$; frozen
kernels: $p=0.44$ and $0.26$). The prior thus brings no measurable gain, and we
make no performance claim for it. Frozen channels could use shifts and additions
instead of multipliers; we have not synthesized this.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Size.} With the regression head, depthwise-separable Blocks~4--6 (0.30M parameters, 265.6M FLOPs),
Block~6 narrowed to 128 channels (0.93M, 404.2M FLOPs) and a multi-scale head
(+0.19M) show no significant difference from the regression head in any metric ($p>0.05$;
the multi-scale head, better in a single-seed pilot, did not hold). Depthwise
Blocks~2--6 of the final model (0.36M parameters, 133M FLOPs) lower mAP@0.5 and
mAP@0.5:0.95 by 2.6 and 3.5 points ($p=0.23$, $0.37$) and recall by 0.7 points
($p=0.004$): an efficiency variant, not the final model.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Checkpoint selection.} We chose checkpoints by validation mAP@0.5, YOLOv8n by
$0.1\,$mAP@0.5$\,+\,0.9\,$mAP@0.5:0.95. With
YOLO's rule, the final model reaches mAP@0.5:0.95 $45.74\pm2.13\%$ (+5.1 points,
$p=0.10$) and mean IoU $71.65\pm1.04\%$, with box recall unchanged ($p=0.82$) and recall
1.0 point lower ($95.44\pm0.97\%$, $p=0.012$): not significant over five seeds, and
still below YOLOv8n ($49.59\%$).

\subsection{Comparison with Lightweight Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

\looseness=-1 At a score threshold of 0.5, EMA-Det accepts $96.46\pm0.81\%$ of the targets,
against $69.94\pm1.64\%$ for a from-scratch and $77.91\pm2.82\%$ for a
COCO-pretrained YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}. This partly reflects the
threshold: at 0.1 YOLOv8n accepts $96.0\%$ and $97.5\%$, but at a precision of
$86.2\%$ and $88.4\%$ against $93.0\%$ for EMA-Det at 0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).
A fair comparison therefore fixes the false-alarm rate. At a $5\%$
false-positive rate, EMA-Det accepts $94.8\pm1.9\%$ of the targets, against
$88.4\pm2.8\%$ and $79.5\pm3.1\%$ (AUROC $0.986\pm0.002$ against
$0.978\pm0.007$ and $0.968\pm0.003$; Welch $t$-test over five seeds, $p<0.01$
except for the AUROC against the COCO-pretrained model, $p=0.07$), with 17
times fewer FLOPs. Localization is closer than before but still behind. EMA-Det's
box recall (Recall$^{\ddagger}$) is $77.6\%$ at 0.5, above YOLOv8n's ($62.8\%$ and
$70.8\%$) and below it at 0.1 ($78.7\%$ against $80.4\%$ and $83.2\%$); its mAP@0.5
is $73.58\%$ against $79.45\%$ and $84.02\%$, and its mAP@0.5:0.95 $40.60\%$ against
$49.59\%$ and $54.10\%$, reflecting YOLOv8n's multi-scale,
distribution-focal-loss (DFL) localization. EMA-Det is thus the better verifier and
a competitive but weaker detector; since the acceptance rate at a given false-alarm
rate bounds system recall, mAP does not predict it.

\looseness=-1 MobileNetV3-Small~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} and ShuffleNetV2
x0.5~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}, trained with the same protocol, are closer. EMA-Det's
AUROC exceeds theirs ($0.980\pm0.003$, $0.975\pm0.003$; $p=0.008$, $0.0006$), and at a
$5\%$ false-positive rate it accepts $94.8\pm1.9\%$ of the targets against $90.4\pm2.1\%$ and
$88.2\pm2.9\%$ ($p=0.014$, $0.007$); their mAP is lower. The price is computation:
483.8M FLOPs, 13 and 19 times those of MobileNetV3-Small (38.0M) and ShuffleNetV2
(24.9M), which also have fewer parameters. Early exit (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}) does not
close this gap, and we have not isolated why EMA-Det separates targets better.

\subsection{Hardware Feasibility Analysis}\label{sec:hardware}

A per-layer translation of the Block1$\to$Gate1$\to$Block4$\to$Gate2 path
into synthesizable C++ for high-level synthesis (HLS), with trained,
BatchNorm-folded weights validated against PyTorch, is in
\texttt{hardware/}. A static bit-width analysis identified on-chip memory as the main
constraint of the Zynq-7020 (630~KB; Block~1 alone used 30.3\%), which
motivated a 3-row line buffer (768~B) instead of a full-frame buffer. Block~1 was synthesized with Vitis Unified IDE 2025.2
(\texttt{xc7z020clg484-1}, 10~ns clock). C-simulation against the PyTorch
\texttt{Block1} reference gives a maximum absolute difference of
$3.3\times10^{-6}$ (\texttt{float}) and $1.48\times10^{-2}$
(\texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>}), consistent with input-quantization noise (the
accumulator uses \texttt{ap\_fixed<32,12>}). Unquantized \texttt{float} needs 784 DSP slices (356\% of the budget), 117{,}654
look-up tables (LUTs, 221\%), 73{,}950 flip-flops (FFs, 69\%) and 12 18-kb
block-RAM tiles (BRAM\_18K, 4\%) at a maximum clock frequency (Fmax) of
137.8~MHz, so it exceeds the DSP and LUT budgets by more than $3\times$ and
$2\times$. \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} at native operand width needs 145 DSPs
(65\%), 13{,}876 LUTs (26\%), 10{,}700 FFs (10\%) and no BRAM at 137.3~MHz:
$2.1\times$ fewer DSPs at equal accuracy, with $37\%$ DSP headroom.

C/register-transfer-level (RTL) co-simulation confirmed that the generated
RTL itself, not only HLS's C-simulation, reproduces this result:
\texttt{2/2} images passed at the same tolerance, with a deterministic
latency of 36{,}489 clock cycles ($\approx$365\,$\mu$s per image,
$\approx$2{,}740 images/s for this kernel alone). One layer is thus
verified numerically, by resource synthesis and by RTL co-simulation.

\looseness=-1 \looseness=-1 This covers only Block~1 (of six blocks plus two gates). It fully unrolls
every multiply--accumulate (MAC) operation (one multiplier per MAC,
initiation interval II$=1$), costing 145 DSPs for 144 MACs per pixel, which
is affordable only with one input channel. Block~2's second convolution
needs $9{,}216$ MACs per pixel ($\sim$42$\times$ the DSP budget under this
strategy) and Block~6's needs $589{,}824$ ($\sim$2{,}680$\times$). Blocks~2--6
therefore require a time-multiplexed MAC array, a systolic or
processing-element (PE) array reused over cycles. This array is not
designed yet: one layer is verified end to end, and the rest of the mapping
is quantified but unsolved. The roadmap follows from these figures.
(i)~\emph{Tiled PE array.} With $T_m$ output $\times$ $T_n$ input channels in
parallel and $T_mT_n\le 220$ DSPs, a layer takes MACs$/(T_mT_n)$
cycles~\cite{zhang2015fpga}; the remaining $\approx$239M MACs (241.9M, half of the
483.8M FLOPs that \texttt{thop} reports, minus Block~1's 2.4M) then bound the latency
from below at $\approx$1.1M cycles ($\approx$8\,ms at 137\,MHz, $\approx$126
images/s), before memory stalls. (ii)~\emph{Weight streaming.} The 1.66M
parameters need $\approx$3.3\,MB at 16 bit ($\approx$1.7\,MB at 8 bit),
against 0.63\,MB of BRAM, so the weights of Blocks~2--6 must be streamed from
external double-data-rate (DDR) memory with double buffering, while
activations stay in line buffers as in Block~1; 8-bit quantization would halve
this traffic at an accuracy cost still to be measured.
(iii)~\emph{Per-block validation.} Each block would repeat Block~1's chain of
C-simulation, synthesis and co-simulation.

\subsection{Cross-Sensor Generalization}\label{sec:crosssensor}

\looseness=-1 To test transfer to an unseen modality, we built an RoI verification
dataset from the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark~\cite{antiuav410}, using
its native video-level split (one video, \texttt{02\_6319}, appeared in both
partitions and was excluded). Positive patches are cropped around the
drone box ($3\times$ context, $128\times128$); negatives are built as in the EO
pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Sampling every 15th frame yields
27{,}768/12{,}388/16{,}808 train/val/test patches.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer.} The five EO-trained final models were evaluated on
the thermal val/test splits without fine-tuning (mean over seeds). Precision stays
high ($93.3\%$ val, $93.1\%$ test), but recall falls from the EO in-domain
$96.46\pm0.81\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:cbam}) to $86.53\pm1.17\%$ (val) and $79.57\pm2.73\%$
(test), and mAP@0.5 from $73.58\%$ to $51.52\pm5.70\%$ and $38.34\pm4.93\%$. The
features keep their discriminative signal, but EO and thermal silhouettes differ
enough to cause many misses.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Thermal-from-scratch reference.} To bound the zero-shot result,
EMA-Det was trained from scratch on the thermal train/val split under
the same protocol ($N{=}5$ seeds): mAP@0.5 $90.73\pm0.42\%$, mAP@0.5:0.95
$56.59\pm0.90\%$, recall $91.89\pm0.29\%$ and precision $97.43\pm0.53\%$.
mAP@0.5 and precision exceed both the zero-shot and the EO in-domain values;
recall exceeds the zero-shot values but stays below the EO in-domain
$96.46\pm0.81\%$. The same network thus reaches 90.7\% mAP@0.5 on thermal data,
which points to domain shift rather than an architectural ceiling.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\looseness=-1 \textbf{Dataset size.} Deduplication leaves 4{,}136 patches
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). \textbf{Cross-sensor generalization.} One
benchmark (Anti-UAV410); no domain adaptation or multi-sensor training.
\textbf{Hardware validation.} Only Block~1 is validated; the time-multiplexed
MAC array for Blocks~2--6 is unsolved (roadmap in Section~\ref{sec:hardware})
and no on-board latency or power is reported. \textbf{Early exit.} It costs
0.3--0.5 recall points (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}), and FPS is a GPU proxy; the
Laplacian/Sobel prior shows no measurable gain (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}). \textbf{Localization.} EMA-Det's mAP is still below YOLOv8n's (about 6 points
in mAP@0.5 from scratch, more in mAP@0.5:0.95), its strict-IoU metrics vary
across seeds, and the head was designed from an error analysis on the EO test split
(EO validation, thermal and external DUT data agree).
\textbf{Recall.} Our recall is an acceptance rate at score 0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}); we also compare at a fixed false-positive rate.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
\looseness=-1 This extended version reports EMA-Det under a controlled protocol ($N{=}5$ seeds,
source-level-split, deduplicated dataset). Decoupling the box head from the
pooled classification feature improves localization and explains most of the
earlier benefit of CBAM. mAP alone is an unreliable guide to verifier quality, since
system-level recall is bounded by the acceptance rate. EMA-Det
separates targets better than MobileNetV3-Small and ShuffleNetV2 at higher
computational cost, and than YOLOv8n at a fixed false-alarm rate with far fewer
FLOPs, but its localization is still weaker. Early exit saves 13.9--21.9\% of
FLOPs for 0.3--0.5 recall points, and the cross-sensor gap is real but not
catastrophic. A synthesized first block and a quantified roadmap scope the
remaining FPGA work.
