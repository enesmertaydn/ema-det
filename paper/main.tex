\title[EMA-Det]{EMA-Det: Hardware-Aware Edge Verification for Two-Stage UAV Detection}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Enes Mert} \sur{Ayd{\i}n}}\email{23050211025@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Osman} \sur{Toklu{\u{g}}lu}}\email{otokluoglu@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Enver} \sur{{\c{C}}avu{\c{s}}}}\email{ecavus@aybu.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering}, \orgname{Ankara Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m Beyaz{\i}t University}, \orgaddress{\city{Ankara}, \country{T{\"u}rkiye}}}

\abstract{Autonomous detection of Low-Slow-Small (LSS) UAVs on edge devices
requires two-stage pipelines, where a lightweight proposal stage nominates
candidate regions that a verification network must confirm or reject under
tight compute and memory budgets. This paper presents EMA-Det, a
hardware-aware region-verification network combining a physically
initialized (Laplacian/Sobel) first layer with two early-exit gates that
let confidently-background candidates skip most of the network, converting
the asymmetric background distribution of candidate regions into a
measurable latency advantage. Under a controlled $N=5$-seed, 150-epoch
protocol on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset, EMA-Det reaches
$96.08\pm0.47\%$ recall, against a full detection architecture (YOLOv8n)
capped at $73.75\pm1.86\%$ under the identical protocol — evidence
supporting this paper's central claim that mAP is not the appropriate
optimization target for a second-stage verifier, since system-level
recall $R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$
is gated by the weaker factor. We further report a static hardware-resource
analysis together with a synthesized, resource-verified FPGA
implementation (Zynq-7020) of the network's first block, motivating and
partially validating the design's departure from FLOPs-oriented
lightweight backbones toward a streaming, memory-bandwidth-aware
architecture. Finally, cross-sensor evaluation on the Anti-UAV410
thermal-IR benchmark shows the EO-trained verifier transfers zero-shot
with precision intact but recall falling to $80.12$--$86.29\%$ and
mAP@0.5 to $39.38$--$52.05\%$, while a thermal-from-scratch reference
under the identical protocol reaches mAP@0.5 $89.52\pm0.93\%$ and recall
$91.64\pm0.41\%$, indicating the zero-shot gap reflects genuine domain
shift rather than an architectural ceiling.}

\keywords{UAV Detection, Region Verification, Early-Exit, Hardware-Aware Design, Cross-Sensor Generalization, Reproducibility}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\textit{This manuscript is an extended version of a paper accepted for
presentation at the IEEE Signal Processing and Communications
Applications Conference (SIU 2026)~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf}; conference
proceedings publication is pending at the time of this submission. This
extended version reports results under a controlled, unified training
protocol (Sections~\ref{sec:cbam} and~\ref{sec:leakage}), adds a decoupled
spatial bounding-box head (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}), and adds a
cross-sensor generalization study (Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}) not
present in the conference version.}

Asymmetric threats posed by Low-Slow-Small (LSS) class Unmanned Aerial
Vehicles (UAVs) to civilian and military critical infrastructure require an
extremely fast and reliable ``sense-decide-act'' loop in modern autonomous
air-defense systems~\cite{luo2021threat,aydin2025aads}. Electro-optical (EO) sensors today
are typically 4K ($3840\times2160$) or higher resolution; processing such
high-resolution video streams directly and in full-frame on edge devices
using standard deep CNNs is infeasible in real time due to bandwidth and
memory constraints~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}.

To alleviate this pixel load, fielded autonomous systems typically use a
two-stage cascade~\cite{fouda2022lightweight}. A first stage (radar priors,
acoustic sensors, or simple motion/background-subtraction algorithms)
identifies candidate Regions of Interest (RoI) in the frame. A second stage
crops these small patches (commonly $64\times64$ or $128\times128$ pixels)
and feeds them to a neural network for verification.

Prior work in this space commonly adapts general-purpose detectors (the
YOLO family, SSD) — originally developed for full-frame optical/aerial
object detection~\cite{li2020object} — to the RoI-verification
task~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. We argue, and demonstrate
empirically, that RoI verification in a two-stage system is \emph{not}
simply a scaled-down detection problem. On edge devices, memory bandwidth is
a more critical bottleneck than raw compute (GFLOPs)~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing};
even edge-compressed YOLO variants (e.g.\ YOLO-Nano) run into resource
limits and energy--performance bottlenecks on FPGA-class
hardware~\cite{adiono2021low,zhang2022apply}. Architectures designed for full-frame
operation, compressed into a micro-input regime, suffer spatial feature loss
from their deep global-pooling stages and asymmetric data flow.

Since total system recall in a two-stage architecture is
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, a
verifier's false negative is a system-level miss. This makes recall, not
mAP, the design-critical metric for the second stage.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) We formalize the RoI-verification problem in
two-stage systems and show empirically that full-frame attention mechanisms
carry a training-stability cost in the micro-input regime rather than a
uniform accuracy penalty (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}). (2) We convert the
asymmetric background distribution of candidate regions (mostly empty sky)
into a latency advantage via an early-exit mechanism in the spirit of prior
confidence-gated early-exit
classifiers~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,liang2024implementation},
adapted here to the two-stage RoI-verification setting. (3) We present a
source-level-split, deduplicated RoI dataset-construction methodology that
enforces a strict train/test boundary at the source-photograph level, and
release it for reuse (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). (4) We report a
controlled, $N=5$-seed comparison under a single unified training protocol
across all ablations and baselines. (5) We show that decoupling the
bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature (Section~
\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) improves localization and accounts for the majority
of CBAM's previously observed benefit. (6) We measure cross-sensor
(EO$\to$thermal-IR) generalization on the Anti-UAV410 benchmark
(Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}), both zero-shot and via a
thermal-from-scratch reference under the same controlled protocol,
quantifying the domain-shift gap directly.

\section{EMA-Det: Architecture and Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System-Level Latency Model}\label{sec:latency}
In a two-stage architecture, total system latency $T_{\text{total}}$ is
modeled as the proposal time plus the sum of per-candidate verification
times (Eq.~\ref{eq:latency}).
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{proposal}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RoI}}} T_{\text{verifier}}^{(i)}
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
When a general-purpose full-frame detector is used as the verifier, every
candidate patch — including empty-sky patches — pays the full network's
fixed cost. EMA-Det's dynamic early-exit structure converts this linear
scaling into an asymptotic one.

\subsection{Laplacian/Sobel-Initialized Kernel Bank}\label{sec:init}
At a constrained $128\times128$ resolution, small targets carry limited
spatial information. Standard Xavier/Kaiming random initialization requires
the network to learn edge boundaries from scratch, increasing iteration cost
and training variance. The first convolution layer's weights $W_1$ are
instead initialized from a deterministic bank of Laplacian ($L$)
\cite{marr1980theory} and Sobel ($S$) \cite{kanopoulos1988design} operators:
\begin{equation}
W^{(0)}_{1,c} = \begin{cases}
K_c, & c \in \{1,\dots,K\} \\
R_c, & c \in \{K+1,\dots,C_{\text{out}}\}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
where $K_c$ are the deterministic physical priors ($L$, $L_2$, $S_x$, $S_y$,
and their signed sums) and $R_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ preserves the
layer's capacity to generalize beyond the fixed operators. These weights
are left unfrozen and are updated during training like any other layer.
The design rationale follows the general inductive-bias argument for
physically motivated initialization: seeding the first layer with
known edge-detection operators is intended to provide the network with a
useful prior over local gradient structure at initialization, rather than
requiring it to be discovered from random weights, which is expected to
be particularly relevant for small targets at $128\times128$ resolution
where edge information is a large fraction of the available signal. We
present this as the motivation for the design choice rather than as an
empirically isolated effect in this study, since we do not report a
controlled convergence-curve comparison against random initialization
under matched seeds and budget; such an ablation is identified as future
work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Block~1 (Section~\ref{sec:init})
reduces $1\times128\times128$ to $16\times64\times64$; Blocks~2--4
progressively reduce to $128\times8\times8$; Block~5 fuses two parallel
dilated branches ($d{=}1,2$) to $128\times4\times4$; Block~6 expands to
$256\times4\times4$; a verification head (global average pool, dropout
$0.4$) produces a classification logit, and a separate spatial branch
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) produces the 4-coordinate bounding-box
refinement.

CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} was evaluated during architecture development and is
\emph{not} part of the default forward path — Section~\ref{sec:cbam}
presents controlled evidence for that design decision.

\subsubsection{Spatial Bounding-Box Head}\label{sec:spatialbbox}
Retaining spatial feature information for box regression, rather than
regressing from a globally pooled classification vector, is standard
practice in modern object-detection architectures. An earlier internal
configuration of the verification head applied global average pooling
before both the classification and box-regression branches, which
discards spatial layout prior to box regression and is a departure from
this convention. We correct this by decoupling the two heads:
classification remains on the pooled vector, while the box head reads the
pre-pooling $256\times4\times4$ feature map directly (flattened, into a
single linear layer), aligning the architecture with standard practice
for spatial box regression. Table~\ref{tab:spatialbbox} reports a
controlled $N{=}5$ ablation quantifying this correction: the spatial head
improves mAP@0.75 (+5.7 points) and mAP@0.5:0.95 (+2.9 points) at a cost
of only 15K additional parameters (${\sim}1\%$) and negligible FLOPs,
while recall is essentially unchanged and its cross-seed standard
deviation decreases (2.54 $\to$ 0.47). It is adopted as the default
architecture throughout this paper; the pooled-vector configuration
remains available (\texttt{use\_spatial\_bbox=False} in
\texttt{ema\_det.py}) for reference.

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}
The verification head is optimized with a combined classification and
box-regression loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, p) + \lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}(\hat{b}, b)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}$ is the Complete-IoU loss~\cite{zheng2020distance}.
In this study, $\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$ and $\lambda_{\text{box}}=2.0$.
Early-exit gates are supervised with their own BCE terms
($\lambda_{\text{gate1}}=0.3$, $\lambda_{\text{gate2}}=0.4$) against the
same binary label as the main head.

\subsection{Dynamic Early-Exit Gates}\label{sec:gates}
Following the confidence-gated early-exit paradigm
(e.g.\ BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}), Gates~1 and~2
(after Blocks~2 and~4) implement the asymptotic rule of
Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}: a candidate halts (classified background, no box
refinement) once its confidence falls below $1-\tau$; otherwise it proceeds.
\begin{equation}
\text{Result} = \begin{cases}
\text{Background (halt)}, & \hat{y}_i \le (1-\tau) \\
C_{i+1}(F_i)\ (\text{continue}), & \hat{y}_i > (1-\tau)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}\label{sec:leakage}

The dataset is built from two public raw sources: rotary-wing and
fixed-wing LSS UAV photographs from a Kaggle
collection~\cite{dasmehdixtr_kaggle} (labeled, 80\% of the raw pool), and
bird/empty-sky photographs from a Roboflow Universe
collection~\cite{mohsen_roboflow} (unlabeled, hard-negative source, 20\%).
The combined raw pool comprises 8{,}793 source photographs (5{,}921
labeled UAV candidates, 2{,}872 sky/negative images).

Because both source collections are web-aggregated and include
sequentially extracted video frames, a substantial fraction of raw
photographs are near-duplicates of one another. To guarantee an
uncontaminated evaluation, we apply an $8\times8$ average-hash comparison
(Hamming distance $\le 5$, clustered via union-find, one representative
retained per cluster, preferring a labeled member) to the raw pool prior
to any patch extraction. This reduces the raw pool to 2{,}142 unique
labeled-candidate photographs and 887 unique sky photographs. The
deduplicated photograph pool is then split into train/val/test
(70/15/15) \emph{before} patch cropping, so that every patch derived from
a given source photograph is guaranteed to fall within that photograph's
assigned split, precluding any source-level overlap between partitions.
One positive patch (around each labeled UAV, $3\times$ context factor)
and one hard-negative patch (from the same photograph, near but not
overlapping the UAV) are then cropped per photograph.

This source-level deduplication and splitting protocol is deliberately
conservative: it prioritizes a strict, non-overlapping evaluation boundary
over raw dataset size. The resulting corpus comprises 4{,}136 patches
(2{,}842 train / 662 val / 632 test), each traceable to a unique source
photograph with no cross-split duplication. All results in this paper are
reported on this dataset; values reported in the conference
version~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf}, which used a patch-level split without
source-level deduplication, are superseded by the values reported here
and should not be compared directly against them.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All models are trained with AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), 5-epoch linear
warmup followed by cosine annealing, batch size 16, gradient clipping at
norm 5.0. Early stopping uses patience 20 on validation mAP@0.5, for up
to 150 epochs. All reported results, including the CBAM ablation, use
$N=5$ seeds $\{42, 7, 123, 2024, 31415\}$ under this identical protocol,
so that architectural comparisons are not confounded by differing
training budgets.

Augmentation excludes Mosaic/MixUp (destroys small-target shape at
$128\times128$); we use horizontal flip, brightness/contrast jitter,
directional motion blur, Gaussian noise, small-angle rotation, and coarse
dropout.

With the spatial bbox head in place, mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75, and mAP@0.5:0.95
are statistically indistinguishable between the two configurations
(differences within one combined standard deviation). CBAM's remaining
measurable cost is recall: $3$ points lower on average
($93.23\pm1.37\%$ vs.\ $96.08\pm0.47\%$) and moderately less stable across
seeds (std $1.37$ vs.\ $0.47$). Under the legacy pooled-vector box head, by
contrast, 2 of 5 CBAM seeds collapsed to 74--79\% recall (std $8.85$); no
such collapse occurs under the spatial-bbox head, where the worst CBAM run
reaches 90.8\% recall.

These results indicate that CBAM's localization contribution under the
pooled-vector head substituted, in part, for spatial information the
pooled feature had discarded; once that information is restored directly
via the spatial bbox head, at negligible parameter and FLOPs cost, CBAM
contributes no additional localization benefit and retains a smaller but
consistent recall cost. On this basis, the final architecture omits CBAM.

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} supports the central claim of
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. YOLOv8n — a full detection architecture with a
multi-scale head and DFL-based box regression — reaches substantially
higher mAP@0.5:0.95 than EMA-Det ($49.59\pm0.45\%$ vs.\
$28.04\pm1.90\%$, ${\sim}1.8\times$), reflecting its localization
advantage even after EMA-Det's spatial bbox head
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) narrows part of the gap. However, YOLOv8n's
recall is capped at $73.75\pm1.86\%$ (tight variance across seeds,
indicating a consistent architectural property rather than seed noise),
well below every other model in the table. Given $R_{\text{total}} =
R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, this recall deficit
illustrates the failure mode this paper argues against: a verifier
optimized for, and evaluated by, mAP can appear stronger on the
commonly reported metric while functioning as a weaker second-stage
verifier under the metric that determines system-level performance. The
two lightweight backbones (MobileNetV3-Small, ShuffleNetV2 x0.5), trained
from scratch with no architectural changes beyond a 1-channel first layer
and the same verification head as EMA-Det, achieve comparable mAP and
recall to EMA-Det despite having 40--77\% fewer parameters and
substantially lower nominal FLOPs (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}); they are
competitive generic backbones for this task but do not provide EMA-Det's
early-exit compute savings (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}) or its recall
stability advantage over CBAM (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}).

This FLOPs disparity motivates, rather than undermines, EMA-Det's use of
standard convolutions in place of depthwise-separable designs. FLOPs are
a proxy for arithmetic cost rather than for hardware efficiency:
depthwise-separable convolutions reduce operation count relative to a
standard convolution by close to $K^2$ (the squared kernel size) but
simultaneously increase feature-map memory traffic by roughly a factor of
two, lowering arithmetic intensity in a way that is directly documented
to complicate efficient mapping onto BRAM-constrained FPGA fabrics such
as the Zynq-7020 and to motivate dedicated depthwise-separable
accelerator designs in the FPGA literature~\cite{zhao2024fpga}; memory
bandwidth, rather than raw compute, is more broadly reported as the
binding constraint for embedded vision targets~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}.
Consistent with this literature, EMA-Det's use of standard convolutions in
a shallow backbone, combined with the early-exit gating of
Section~\ref{sec:gates}, is motivated by targeting streaming memory
throughput on the intended edge/FPGA target rather than by minimizing
nominal GFLOPs. We report this as the design rationale underlying
EMA-Det's convolution choice; this study does not synthesize
MobileNetV3-Small or ShuffleNetV2 to FPGA hardware, and
Section~\ref{sec:hardware} accordingly restricts its resource-utilization
comparison to EMA-Det's own Block~1 rather than to these baselines.

\subsection{Hardware Feasibility Analysis}\label{sec:hardware}

This section reports measured results together with a static resource
analysis used to scope the network's FPGA mapping. Both configurations
(final and CBAM, spatial-bbox architecture, Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox})
were exported to ONNX and verified against their PyTorch models (maximum
absolute difference $1.7\times10^{-5}$ / $1.3\times10^{-5}$ over 20 random
inputs). Inference throughput was measured on a development machine
(RTX~4060 laptop GPU with host CPU) at batch size 1, matching the
single-RoI streaming pattern of the target edge deployment. Under this
setting, PyTorch-CUDA inference reaches 378.7 FPS (final) / 248.8 FPS
(CBAM); ONNX Runtime's CPU execution provider reaches 738.1 / 646.5 FPS
respectively, exceeding the GPU figures. This inversion is expected rather
than anomalous: GPU throughput gains are realized primarily through batch
parallelism, and a single-sample, sequential early-exit workload — where
each candidate RoI is gated and, in a large fraction of cases, exits after
one or two blocks (Table~\ref{tab:earlyexit}) — does not present the
uniform, large-batch workload under which GPU throughput is
maximized. Accordingly, the CPU/ONNX Runtime figures, together with the
static resource analysis and FPGA synthesis results below, are treated as
the more representative reference points for the intended streaming edge
deployment; the GPU figures are reported for completeness rather than as
the basis for the paper's hardware-efficiency claims. The spatial bbox
head's additional flatten-and-linear layer carries a measured throughput
cost relative to the legacy head (465.0 FPS (final) / 262.6 FPS (CBAM)
under the same batch-1 GPU setting), which is reported alongside its
accuracy gain (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) for completeness.

A per-layer translation of the Block1$\to$Gate1$\to$Block4$\to$Gate2 path
into synthesizable C++, using the trained (BatchNorm-folded) weights and
validated against PyTorch reference outputs on real test images, is
provided in \texttt{hardware/}. A static element-count$\times$bit-width
analysis (\texttt{src/estimate\_hls\_bram.py}) of this direct,
fully-buffered translation identified on-chip memory as the primary
constraint for the target Zynq-7020 (630~KB BRAM budget), with Block~1
alone accounting for 30.3\% of the estimated total. This analysis directly
informed the streaming redesign described below.

Block~1 was restructured around a 3-row line buffer (768~B, replacing the
full-frame buffer) and synthesized with Vitis Unified IDE 2025.2
(Zynq-7020, part \texttt{xc7z020clg484-1}, 10~ns clock target).
C-simulation against the PyTorch \texttt{Block1} reference output
(post-LeakyReLU, two test images, $16\times128\times128$ values each)
confirms numerical correctness in both representations: maximum absolute
difference $3.3\times10^{-6}$ in \texttt{float} and
$1.48\times10^{-2}$ in the target \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} fixed-point
representation, consistent with expected input-activation quantization
noise (weights and the MAC accumulator use a wider intermediate type,
\texttt{ap\_fixed<32,12>}). Table~\ref{tab:block1synth} reports the
resulting resource utilization for \emph{both} representations, since the
contrast is itself informative: the unquantized \texttt{float} build
already fits the BRAM budget (line buffering alone fixes that) but
overshoots DSP and LUT by more than $3\times$ and $2\times$ respectively —
floating-point arithmetic is simply far more expensive in hardware than
fixed-point, independent of buffering strategy. Quantizing to
\texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} (and, separately, multiplying at native 16-bit
width rather than in the wider accumulator type — an earlier attempt that
multiplied pre-widened operands cost $2.1\times$ more DSPs for no accuracy
benefit) brings every resource under budget, with $37\%$ DSP headroom
remaining.

C/RTL co-simulation subsequently confirmed that the generated RTL itself
(not merely HLS's own C-simulation of the pre-synthesis source) reproduces
this result: \texttt{2/2} images passed with the same tolerance, at a
measured, deterministic latency of 36{,}489 clock cycles
($\approx$365\,$\mu$s/image at the 10~ns clock target, $\approx$2{,}740
images/s for this kernel alone). This is a real, tool-verified result for
one layer, not an estimate — numerically confirmed in both float and
fixed point, resource-budget-confirmed by real synthesis, and now
RTL-confirmed by real co-simulation.

This result covers only Block~1 (one of six blocks plus two gates),
however, and its approach does not simply repeat for the rest of the
network. Block~1's kernel fully unrolls every MAC (one physical
multiplier per multiply-accumulate, pipelined at II$=1$), which cost 145
DSPs for its $16\times1\times3\times3=144$ MACs/pixel — affordable only
because Block~1 has just one input channel. MACs/pixel grow with channel
depth at every subsequent layer: Block~2's second conv already needs
$32\times32\times9=9{,}216$ MACs/pixel ($\sim$42$\times$ the entire
chip's DSP budget at the same one-per-MAC strategy), and Block~6's second
conv needs $256\times256\times9=589{,}824$ ($\sim$2{,}680$\times$). Block~2
onward therefore require a fundamentally different structure — a small,
fixed-size, time-multiplexed MAC array (a systolic/PE-array design reused
over many cycles per output, trading latency for area) rather than full
unrolling — which has not yet been designed for any layer past Block~1.
We report this status transparently: one layer's line-buffer
restructuring is fully verified end-to-end, the rest of the network's
hardware mapping is identified, quantified, and unsolved, not silently
assumed to generalize.

\subsection{Cross-Sensor Generalization}\label{sec:crosssensor}

To assess how EMA-Det's EO-domain performance transfers to an unseen
sensor modality, we constructed an RoI verification dataset from the
Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark~\cite{antiuav410}, using its native
video-level train/val/test split (frames from a given video remain within
a single partition). One video (\texttt{02\_6319}) was found to span both
partitions under non-overlapping frame ranges and was excluded from both
as a precaution. Positive patches were cropped around the annotated drone
box (context multiplier $3\times$, resized to $128\times128$); negatives
combine drone-absent frames with same-frame hard negatives cropped near
the drone but not overlapping it, mirroring the EO pipeline's
negative-mining strategy (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Every 15th frame of
each video was sampled to limit near-duplicate adjacent frames. The
resulting dataset comprises 27{,}768 / 12{,}388 / 16{,}808 train/val/test
patches.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer.} The EO-trained final checkpoint
(spatial-bbox, no CBAM, Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) was evaluated
directly on the thermal val/test splits without fine-tuning.
Table~\ref{tab:crosssensor} reports the result. Precision remains high
(95.3--95.5\%), while recall falls from the EO in-domain
$96.08\pm0.47\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:cbam}) to 80--86\% on thermal data, and
mAP@0.5 falls to 39--52\%. This indicates that the verifier's
Laplacian/Sobel-initialized first layer and learned features retain
discriminative UAV/non-UAV signal across modalities without exposure to
thermal data, while the visual distribution shift between EO and
thermal-IR silhouettes accounts for a non-trivial fraction of missed
detections.

thermal-IR UAV signatures presenting as comparatively high-contrast,
low-clutter targets relative to EO imagery.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dataset size.} The source-level deduplication and splitting
protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:leakage} yields 4{,}136 patches
from an initial raw pool of 8{,}793 source photographs. This is a
deliberate trade-off favoring evaluation rigor over raw sample count;
expanding the raw source pool with additional, verified-non-duplicate UAV
photography would allow a larger dataset under the same protocol and is
identified as future work.
\item \textbf{Cross-sensor generalization.} Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}
reports zero-shot EO$\to$thermal transfer and a thermal-from-scratch
reference on a single benchmark (Anti-UAV410); domain adaptation and
multi-sensor joint training were not evaluated and are natural extensions
given the from-scratch result.
\item \textbf{Hardware validation.} Section~\ref{sec:hardware} reports a
synthesized, resource-verified, and C/RTL-cosimulation-verified
implementation (float and \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>}) of Block~1 against
Zynq-7020 resource budgets. The time-multiplexed MAC-array design
required for Blocks~2 through~6 remains future work; no other layer has
been synthesized.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This extended version reports EMA-Det under a controlled, $N=5$-seed
protocol on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset. Decoupling the
bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature improves
localization and accounts for the majority of CBAM's previously observed
benefit, indicating that CBAM's contribution under a pooled-feature head
substituted for spatial information rather than adding an independent
localization benefit. Comparison against a full detection architecture
(YOLOv8n) and lightweight backbones establishes that mAP alone is an
unreliable indicator of second-stage verifier quality, since recall — not
mAP — determines system-level detection performance under a two-stage
cascade. Cross-sensor evaluation on Anti-UAV410 quantifies a genuine but
non-catastrophic EO$\to$thermal domain-shift gap. A hardware feasibility
analysis, including a synthesized and resource-verified implementation of
the network's first block, scopes the remaining work required for a full
FPGA mapping. We release the dataset-construction and evaluation tooling
(\texttt{build\_dataset\_clean.py}, \texttt{check\_leakage.py},
\texttt{check\_raw\_duplicates.py}) to support reproducible construction of
similar two-stage RoI-verification datasets from web-aggregated source
imagery.
