\title[EMA-Det]{EMA-Det: Hardware-Aware Edge Verification for Two-Stage UAV Detection}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Enes Mert} \sur{Ayd{\i}n}}\email{23050211025@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Osman} \sur{Toklu{\u{g}}lu}}\email{otokluoglu@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Enver} \sur{{\c{C}}avu{\c{s}}}}\email{ecavus@aybu.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering}, \orgname{Ankara Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m Beyaz{\i}t University}, \orgaddress{\city{Ankara}, \country{T{\"u}rkiye}}}

\abstract{Autonomous detection of Low-Slow-Small (LSS) UAVs on edge devices typically relies on two-stage pipelines, where a lightweight proposal stage nominates candidate regions that a verification network must evaluate under strict computational and memory constraints. This paper presents EMA-Det, a hardware-aware region-verification network for this second stage, integrating a physically initialized (Laplacian/Sobel) first layer with two early-exit gates that let high-confidence background regions bypass most network layers, translating the asymmetric candidate-region distribution into a measurable latency advantage. Evaluated on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset under a controlled $N{=}5$-seed, 150-epoch protocol, EMA-Det achieves $96.08\pm0.47\%$ recall, versus $73.75\pm1.86\%$ for a full detection baseline (YOLOv8n) under identical conditions -- demonstrating that mean Average Precision (mAP) is a suboptimal optimization target for a second-stage verifier, since system-level recall $R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$ is bottlenecked by the weaker factor. To validate edge-deployment viability, we present a static hardware-resource analysis alongside a synthesized, resource-verified FPGA implementation (Zynq-7020) of the network's first block, substantiating a shift from FLOPs-oriented lightweight backbones toward a streaming, memory-bandwidth-aware architecture. Finally, cross-sensor evaluation on the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark shows the EO-trained verifier transfers zero-shot with precision intact but recall falling to $80.12$--$86.29\%$ and mAP@0.5 to $39.38$--$52.05\%$, while EMA-Det trained from scratch on thermal data achieves mAP@0.5 $89.52\pm0.93\%$ and recall $91.64\pm0.41\%$ -- confirming the zero-shot gap reflects genuine domain shift rather than an architectural ceiling.}

\keywords{LSS UAV Detection, Region Verification, Early-Exit, FPGA, Edge Computing, Cross-Sensor Generalization}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\textit{This manuscript extends a paper accepted at IEEE SIU
2026~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf} (proceedings pending), adding a
controlled, unified training protocol (Sections~\ref{sec:cbam}
and~\ref{sec:leakage}), a decoupled spatial bounding-box head
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}), and a cross-sensor generalization study
(Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}) absent from the conference version.}

Asymmetric threats posed by Low-Slow-Small (LSS) class Unmanned Aerial
Vehicles (UAVs) to civilian and military infrastructure require a fast,
reliable ``sense-decide-act'' loop in autonomous
air-defense systems~\cite{luo2021threat,aydin2025aads}. EO sensors today
are typically 4K ($3840\times2160$) or higher; processing such streams
directly and in full-frame on edge devices using standard deep CNNs is
infeasible in real time due to bandwidth and memory
constraints~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}. Fielded autonomous systems
therefore typically use a two-stage
cascade~\cite{kiefer2023roi}: a first stage (radar
priors~\cite{nabati2019rrpn}, acoustic sensors~\cite{tegler2025sound}, or
motion/background-subtraction) identifies candidate RoIs, and a second
stage crops small patches and feeds them to a network for verification.

Prior work commonly adapts general-purpose detectors (the YOLO family,
SSD) — developed for full-frame optical/aerial
detection~\cite{li2020object} — to the RoI-verification
task~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. We argue, and
demonstrate empirically, that RoI verification is \emph{not} simply a
scaled-down detection problem: memory bandwidth is a more critical
edge-device bottleneck than raw
compute~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}, and edge-compressed YOLO variants
run into resource and energy--performance bottlenecks on FPGA-class
hardware~\cite{adiono2021low,zhang2022apply}. Full-frame architectures,
compressed into a micro-input regime, suffer spatial feature loss from
deep global-pooling and asymmetric data flow. Since total system recall
is $R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$,
a verifier's false negative is a system-level miss, making recall —
not mAP — the design-critical metric for the second stage.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) We formalize the RoI-verification problem
and show full-frame attention carries a training-stability cost in the
micro-input regime, not a uniform accuracy penalty (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}).
(2) We convert the asymmetric background distribution of candidate
regions into a latency advantage via an early-exit mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). (3) We present a source-level-split,
deduplicated RoI dataset-construction methodology enforcing a strict
train/test boundary, released for reuse (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).
(4) We report a controlled, $N{=}5$-seed comparison under one unified
protocol across all ablations and baselines. (5) We show decoupling the
bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) improves localization and accounts for
most of CBAM's observed benefit. (6) We measure cross-sensor
(EO$\to$thermal-IR) generalization on Anti-UAV410
(Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}), zero-shot and from-scratch,
quantifying the domain-shift gap.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Two-stage detection and efficient backbones.} Two-stage
detectors separate proposal from verification, trading latency for
accuracy relative to single-stage designs~\cite{ren2015faster}; cascade
architectures refine localization through successive, increasing-IoU
stages~\cite{cai2018cascade}. UAV-specific work follows the same
system-level split~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}; EMA-Det
instead targets the verification stage specifically, via
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$
(Section~\ref{sec:intro}) rather than as a scaled-down general detector.
Separately, mobile-oriented backbones cut FLOPs via depthwise-separable
or grouped convolutions~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} and
channel-shuffle~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}, optimized against mobile-CPU
cost models; FPGA accelerator work shows these operations instead raise
memory-bandwidth pressure on BRAM-constrained
fabrics~\cite{zhao2024fpga} — consistent with EMA-Det's shallow,
streaming-friendly backbone of standard convolutions
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\paragraph{Early-exit inference and attention.} Confidence-gated early
exits let easy inputs skip later
stages~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,liang2024implementation},
surveyed in~\cite{rahmath2024earlyexit}, generally for classification
with no strong class prior; EMA-Det's gates (Section~\ref{sec:gates})
instead exploit the RoI-verification setting's background-majority
asymmetry. Separately, channel-spatial attention (CBAM) recalibrates
features at negligible cost~\cite{woo2018cbam}, and stride-free
downsampling has been proposed to preserve small-object spatial
information lost to strided pooling~\cite{sunkara2022spdconv};
Sections~\ref{sec:cbam} and~\ref{sec:spatialbbox} reach a related
conclusion via a decoupled spatial bbox head rather than attention
alone.

\paragraph{Cross-sensor UAV detection.} Cross-modality UAV detection has
been studied as EO/IR fusion~\cite{sun2022dronevehicle} and, for the
benchmark used here, as single-sensor thermal-IR
detection~\cite{antiuav410}. This paper does not fuse modalities;
Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor} measures the zero-shot EO$\to$thermal gap
directly.

\section{EMA-Det: Architecture and Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System-Level Latency Model}\label{sec:latency}
In a two-stage architecture, total system latency $T_{\text{total}}$ is
modeled as the proposal time plus the sum of per-candidate verification
times (Eq.~\ref{eq:latency}).
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{proposal}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RoI}}} T_{\text{verifier}}^{(i)}
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
When a general-purpose full-frame detector is used as the verifier, every
candidate patch — including empty-sky patches — pays the full network's
fixed cost. EMA-Det's dynamic early-exit structure converts this linear
scaling into an asymptotic one.

\subsection{Laplacian/Sobel-Initialized Kernel Bank}\label{sec:init}
At a constrained $128\times128$ resolution, small targets carry limited
spatial information. Standard Xavier/Kaiming random initialization requires
the network to learn edge boundaries from scratch, increasing iteration cost
and training variance. The first convolution layer's weights $W_1$ are
instead initialized from a deterministic bank of Laplacian ($L$)
\cite{marr1980theory} and Sobel ($S$) \cite{kanopoulos1988design} operators:
\begin{equation}
W^{(0)}_{1,c} = \begin{cases}
K_c, & c \in \{1,\dots,K\} \\
R_c, & c \in \{K+1,\dots,C_{\text{out}}\}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
where $K_c$ are the deterministic physical priors ($L$, $L_2$, $S_x$, $S_y$,
and their signed sums) and $R_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ preserves the
layer's capacity to generalize beyond the fixed operators. These weights
are left unfrozen and are updated during training like any other layer.
The rationale follows the inductive-bias argument for physically
motivated initialization: seeding the first layer with known
edge-detection operators provides a useful prior over local gradient
structure rather than requiring it be discovered from random weights —
expected to matter for small targets at $128\times128$ resolution,
where edge information is a large fraction of the available signal.
This is motivation for the design choice, not an isolated effect; a
controlled convergence-curve comparison against random initialization
is not reported here (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Block~1 (Section~\ref{sec:init})
reduces $1\times128\times128$ to $16\times64\times64$; Blocks~2--4
progressively reduce to $128\times8\times8$; Block~5 fuses two parallel
dilated branches ($d{=}1,2$) to $128\times4\times4$; Block~6 expands to
$256\times4\times4$; a verification head (global average pool, dropout
$0.4$) produces a classification logit, and a separate spatial branch
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) produces the 4-coordinate bounding-box
refinement.

CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} was evaluated during architecture development and is
\emph{not} part of the default forward path — Section~\ref{sec:cbam}
presents controlled evidence for that design decision.

\subsubsection{Spatial Bounding-Box Head}\label{sec:spatialbbox}
Retaining spatial feature information for box regression, rather than
regressing from a globally pooled classification vector, is standard
practice in modern detection architectures~\cite{ren2015faster,liu2016ssd,lin2017focal,tian2019fcos}.
An earlier internal
configuration pooled before both branches, discarding spatial layout
prior to box regression. This is corrected: classification remains on
the pooled vector, while the box head reads the pre-pooling
$256\times4\times4$ feature map directly (flattened into a single
linear layer). Table~\ref{tab:cbam}'s legacy row reports a controlled
$N{=}5$ ablation against the pooled-vector config: the spatial head
improves mAP@0.75 (+5.7 points) and mAP@0.5:0.95 (+2.9 points) at a cost
of only 15K parameters (${\sim}1\%$) and negligible FLOPs, while recall
is essentially unchanged and its cross-seed std decreases
($2.54\to0.47$); it is the default throughout this paper, with the
pooled-vector configuration remaining available
(\texttt{use\_spatial\_bbox=False}).

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}
The verification head combines classification and box-regression terms
(Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, p) + \lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}(\hat{b}, b)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}$ is the Complete-IoU loss~\cite{zheng2020distance},
$\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$, and $\lambda_{\text{box}}=2.0$. Early-exit gates
add their own BCE terms ($\lambda_{\text{gate1}}=0.3$, $\lambda_{\text{gate2}}=0.4$)
against the same binary label.

\subsection{Dynamic Early-Exit Gates}\label{sec:gates}
Following the confidence-gated early-exit paradigm
(e.g.\ BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}), Gates~1 and~2
(after Blocks~2 and~4) implement the asymptotic rule of
Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}: a candidate halts (classified background, no box
refinement) once its confidence falls below $1-\tau$; otherwise it proceeds.
\begin{equation}
\text{Result} = \begin{cases}
\text{Background (halt)}, & \hat{y}_i \le (1-\tau) \\
C_{i+1}(F_i)\ (\text{continue}), & \hat{y}_i > (1-\tau)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}\label{sec:leakage}

The dataset is built from two public raw sources: rotary-wing and
fixed-wing LSS UAV photographs from a Kaggle
collection~\cite{dasmehdixtr_kaggle} (labeled, 80\% of the raw pool), and
bird/empty-sky photographs from a Roboflow Universe
collection~\cite{mohsen_roboflow} (unlabeled, hard-negative source, 20\%).
The combined raw pool comprises 8{,}793 source photographs (5{,}921
labeled UAV candidates, 2{,}872 sky/negative images).

Both source collections are web-aggregated and include sequentially
extracted video frames, so a substantial fraction of raw photographs are
near-duplicates. To guarantee an uncontaminated evaluation, an
$8\times8$ average-hash comparison (Hamming distance $\le 5$, clustered
via union-find, one representative retained per cluster, preferring a
labeled member) is applied to the raw pool prior to patch extraction,
reducing it to 2{,}142 unique labeled-candidate and 887 unique sky
photographs. This deduplicated pool is split into train/val/test
(70/15/15) \emph{before} patch cropping, so every patch inherits its
source photograph's split, precluding source-level overlap. One positive
patch ($3\times$ context factor) and one hard-negative patch (same
photograph, near but not overlapping the UAV) are cropped per photograph.

This protocol is deliberately conservative, prioritizing a strict
evaluation boundary over raw dataset size. The resulting corpus comprises
4{,}136 patches (2{,}842/662/632 train/val/test), each traceable to a
unique source photograph with no cross-split duplication. All results
here use this dataset; values in the conference
version~\cite{aydin2026emadetconf}, which used a patch-level split without
source-level deduplication, are superseded and should not be compared
directly.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All models are trained with AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), 5-epoch
linear warmup, cosine annealing, batch size 16, and gradient clipping
(norm 5.0), with early stopping (patience 20 on val.\ mAP@0.5, up to 150
epochs). All results, including the CBAM ablation, use $N{=}5$ seeds
$\{42, 7, 123, 2024, 31415\}$ under this identical protocol. Augmentation
excludes Mosaic/MixUp (destroys small-target shape at $128\times128$);
we use horizontal flip, brightness/contrast jitter, motion blur,
Gaussian noise, small-angle rotation, and coarse dropout.

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} supports the central claim of
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} reaches
substantially higher mAP@0.5:0.95 than EMA-Det ($49.59\pm0.45\%$ vs.\
$28.04\pm1.90\%$, ${\sim}1.8\times$), reflecting its multi-scale,
DFL-based localization advantage even after the spatial bbox head
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) narrows part of the gap. Its recall is
capped at $73.75\pm1.86\%$ (tight cross-seed variance, an architectural
property, not seed noise), well below every other model. Given
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$,
this illustrates the failure mode this paper argues against: a verifier
optimized for, and evaluated by, mAP can appear stronger on the commonly
reported metric while being weaker under the metric that determines
system-level performance. The two lightweight backbones
(MobileNetV3-Small~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3}, ShuffleNetV2
x0.5~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}), trained from scratch with the same
head as EMA-Det, achieve comparable mAP/recall despite 40--77\% fewer
parameters and lower FLOPs; they lack EMA-Det's early-exit savings
(Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}) or its recall stability advantage over
CBAM (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}). This FLOPs disparity motivates, rather
than undermines, EMA-Det's standard convolutions over
depthwise-separable designs: FLOPs proxy arithmetic cost rather than
hardware efficiency, and depthwise-separable convolutions raise
memory-bandwidth pressure on BRAM-constrained FPGA fabrics despite
lower operation count (Section~\ref{sec:related})~\cite{zhao2024fpga,qasaimeh2019comparing}.
This study does not synthesize MobileNetV3-Small or ShuffleNetV2 to
FPGA hardware, so Section~\ref{sec:hardware} restricts its
resource-utilization comparison to EMA-Det's own Block~1.

\subsection{Hardware Feasibility Analysis}\label{sec:hardware}

This section reports measured results together with a static resource
analysis scoping the network's FPGA mapping. Both configurations were
exported to ONNX and verified against PyTorch (max.\ absolute difference
$1.7\times10^{-5}$/$1.3\times10^{-5}$, 20 random inputs). At batch size 1,
PyTorch-CUDA reaches 378.7/248.8 FPS (final/CBAM) while ONNX Runtime's
CPU provider reaches 738.1/646.5 FPS — higher than GPU, since GPU gains
need batch parallelism that a sequential, early-exit-gated single-RoI
workload (Table~\ref{tab:earlyexit}) does not provide. The CPU/ONNX and
FPGA figures below are thus the representative edge-deployment
reference points. The spatial bbox head costs some throughput over the
legacy head (465.0/262.6 FPS, same setting), alongside its accuracy
gain (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}).

A per-layer translation of the Block1$\to$Gate1$\to$Block4$\to$Gate2 path
into synthesizable C++ (trained, BatchNorm-folded weights, validated
against PyTorch) is in \texttt{hardware/}. A static bit-width analysis
(\texttt{src/estimate\_hls\_bram.py}) of this fully-buffered translation
identified on-chip memory as the primary constraint for the Zynq-7020
(630~KB budget), Block~1 alone accounting for 30.3\% of the total,
informing the streaming redesign below: a 3-row line buffer (768~B,
replacing the full-frame buffer), synthesized with Vitis Unified IDE
2025.2 (Zynq-7020, \texttt{xc7z020clg484-1}, 10~ns clock target).
C-simulation against the PyTorch \texttt{Block1} reference confirms
numerical correctness in both representations: max.\ absolute
difference $3.3\times10^{-6}$ (\texttt{float}) and $1.48\times10^{-2}$
(\texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>}), consistent with input-activation
quantization noise (accumulator uses the wider \texttt{ap\_fixed<32,12>}).
Table~\ref{tab:block1synth} reports both: unquantized \texttt{float}
fits the BRAM budget but overshoots DSP/LUT by more than
$3\times$/$2\times$ — floating-point is far more expensive in hardware
than fixed-point. Quantizing to \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} and multiplying
at native width rather than pre-widened operands ($2.1\times$ fewer
DSPs, equal accuracy) brings every resource under budget, with $37\%$
DSP headroom.

C/RTL co-simulation subsequently confirmed that the generated RTL itself
(not merely HLS's C-simulation of the pre-synthesis source) reproduces
this result: \texttt{2/2} images passed at the same tolerance, with a
measured, deterministic latency of 36{,}489 clock cycles
($\approx$365\,$\mu$s/image, $\approx$2{,}740 images/s for this kernel
alone). This is a real, tool-verified result for one layer — confirmed
numerically, by resource synthesis, and by RTL co-simulation.

This result covers only Block~1 (of six blocks plus two gates): it
fully unrolls every MAC (one multiplier per MAC, II$=1$), costing 145
DSPs for 144 MACs/pixel — affordable only with one input channel.
MACs/pixel scale with channel depth: Block~2's second conv needs
$9{,}216$ MACs/pixel ($\sim$42$\times$ the chip's DSP budget under the
same strategy), Block~6's needs $589{,}824$ ($\sim$2{,}680$\times$), so
Block~2 onward require a time-multiplexed MAC array (systolic/PE-array,
reused over cycles) rather than full unrolling — not yet designed past
Block~1. One layer is thus fully verified end-to-end; the rest of the
network's hardware mapping is quantified but unsolved.

\subsection{Cross-Sensor Generalization}\label{sec:crosssensor}

To assess EO-domain transfer to an unseen modality, an RoI verification
dataset was built from the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR
benchmark~\cite{antiuav410}, using its native video-level split (one
video, \texttt{02\_6319}, spanned both partitions and was excluded).
Positive patches were cropped around the annotated drone box
($3\times$ context, resized to $128\times128$); negatives combine
drone-absent frames with same-frame hard negatives, mirroring the EO
pipeline's negative-mining strategy (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Every
15th frame was sampled to limit near-duplicates, yielding
27{,}768/12{,}388/16{,}808 train/val/test patches.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer.} The EO-trained final checkpoint
(spatial-bbox, no CBAM) was evaluated directly on the thermal val/test
splits without fine-tuning (Table~\ref{tab:crosssensor}). Precision
stays high (95.3--95.5\%), while recall falls from the EO in-domain
$96.08\pm0.47\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:cbam}) to 80--86\%, and mAP@0.5 to
39--52\% — the verifier's learned features retain discriminative
UAV/non-UAV signal across modalities, while EO/thermal-IR silhouette
shift accounts for a non-trivial fraction of missed detections.

\textbf{Thermal-from-scratch reference.} To bound the zero-shot result,
EMA-Det (final) was trained from scratch on the thermal train/val split

mAP@0.5 $89.52\pm0.93\%$, recall $91.64\pm0.41\%$, precision

the same network (1{,}532{,}391 params, 423.085M FLOPs) reaches 89.5\%
mAP@0.5 trained directly on thermal data, establishing genuine domain
shift rather than an architectural ceiling. Higher mAP/precision on
thermal data is consistent with thermal-IR signatures presenting as
high-contrast, low-clutter targets relative to EO imagery.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Dataset size:} deduplication (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) yields
4{,}136 patches from 8{,}793 raw photographs, trading sample count for
evaluation rigor; expanding the raw pool is future work.
\textbf{Cross-sensor generalization:} Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}
reports zero-shot/from-scratch results on one benchmark (Anti-UAV410);
domain adaptation and multi-sensor training were not evaluated.
\textbf{Hardware validation:} Section~\ref{sec:hardware}
synthesizes/cosimulates Block~1 only; the time-multiplexed MAC array
for Blocks~2--6 remains future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This extended version reports EMA-Det under a controlled, $N{=}5$-seed
protocol on a source-level-split, deduplicated dataset. Decoupling the
bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature improves
localization and accounts for most of CBAM's previously observed benefit
— indicating that benefit substituted for discarded spatial information
rather than an independent localization gain. Comparison against a full
detection architecture (YOLOv8n) and lightweight backbones establishes
that mAP alone is an unreliable indicator of verifier quality, since
recall — not mAP — determines system-level performance under a
two-stage cascade. Cross-sensor evaluation on Anti-UAV410 quantifies a
genuine but non-catastrophic EO$\to$thermal domain-shift gap, and a
hardware feasibility analysis — including a synthesized,
resource-verified implementation of the network's first block — scopes
the work remaining for a full FPGA mapping. We release the
dataset-construction and evaluation tooling
(\texttt{build\_dataset\_clean.py}, \texttt{check\_leakage.py},
\texttt{check\_raw\_duplicates.py}) to support reproducible construction
of similar datasets from web-aggregated source imagery.
