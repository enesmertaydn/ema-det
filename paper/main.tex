\title[EMA-Det]{EMA-Det: Hardware-Aware Edge Verification for Two-Stage UAV Detection}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Enes Mert} \sur{Ayd{\i}n}}\email{23050211025@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Osman} \sur{Toklu{\u{g}}lu}}\email{otokluoglu@aybu.edu.tr}

\author[1]{\fnm{Enver} \sur{{\c{C}}avu{\c{s}}}}\email{ecavus@aybu.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering}, \orgname{Ankara Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m Beyaz{\i}t University}, \orgaddress{\city{Ankara}, \country{T{\"u}rkiye}}}

\abstract{Autonomous detection of Low-Slow-Small (LSS) UAVs on edge devices necessitates
two-stage pipelines, in which a lightweight proposal stage nominates
candidate regions that a verification network must confirm or reject under
tight compute and memory budgets. This paper presents EMA-Det, a
hardware-aware region-verification network combining a physically
initialized (Laplacian/Sobel) first layer with two early-exit gates that let
confidently-background candidates skip the majority of the network. We
identify and correct two methodological issues present in our earlier
conference report: (i) a confound between an attention-module (CBAM)
ablation and the main comparison table, caused by the two being trained
under different budgets, and (ii) train/test leakage in the region-patch
dataset, traced to near-duplicate raw source photographs (consistent with
sequential video-frame extraction) being split at the patch level rather
than the source-photograph level. Correcting both, we retrain EMA-Det with
and without CBAM under an identical $N=5$-seed, 150-epoch protocol on a
deduplicated, source-level-split dataset, and further decouple the box head
from the pooled classification feature so it retains positional information
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}), which strictly improves localization
(mAP@0.5:0.95: $25.18\pm2.08\% \to 28.04\pm1.90\%$) at negligible cost.
Under the original (pooled-vector) box head, CBAM appeared to trade a
localization gain for a severe reliability cost (recall std $8.85$ vs.\
$2.54$ points, 2 of 5 seeds collapsing to 74--79\% recall). Once the box
head is fixed to retain spatial information, that entire trade-off
collapses: CBAM's mAP advantage vanishes (mAP@0.5:0.95 $28.30\pm2.07\%$ vs.\
$28.04\pm1.90\%$, effectively tied) while its recall is still lower and
somewhat less stable ($93.23\pm1.37\%$ vs.\ $96.08\pm0.47\%$) — indicating
CBAM's original benefit was substituting for spatial information the
pooled bbox head had been discarding, not adding anything the fixed
architecture cannot already provide on its own. This is a cleaner
justification for excluding CBAM than either of our earlier analyses gave.
Against lightweight baselines trained from scratch on the same corrected
dataset, a full detection architecture (YOLOv8n) reaches substantially
higher mAP@0.5:0.95 ($49.59\pm0.45\%$) but its recall is capped at
$73.75\pm1.86\%$ — well below EMA-Det's $96.08\pm0.47\%$ — concretely
illustrating this paper's central claim that mAP is the wrong optimization
target for a second-stage verifier, since system-level recall
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$ is
gated by the weaker of the two factors. Finally, we evaluate cross-sensor
generalization on the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark: the EO-trained
verifier transfers zero-shot with precision intact but recall falling from
$96.08\pm0.47\%$ to $80.12$--$86.29\%$ and mAP@0.5 to $39.38$--$52.05\%$,
while a thermal-from-scratch reference under the identical protocol
reaches mAP@0.5 $89.52\pm0.93\%$ and recall $91.64\pm0.41\%$ — confirming
the zero-shot gap is a genuine domain-shift effect rather than an
architectural ceiling, and suggesting thermal-IR verification is, if
anything, an easier task than EO at this resolution.}

\keywords{UAV Detection, Region Verification, Early-Exit, Hardware-Aware Design, Cross-Sensor Generalization, Reproducibility}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Asymmetric threats posed by Low-Slow-Small (LSS) class Unmanned Aerial
Vehicles (UAVs) to civilian and military critical infrastructure require an
extremely fast and reliable ``sense-decide-act'' loop in modern autonomous
air-defense systems~\cite{luo2021threat}. Electro-optical (EO) sensors today
are typically 4K ($3840\times2160$) or higher resolution; processing such
high-resolution video streams directly and in full-frame on edge devices
using standard deep CNNs is infeasible in real time due to bandwidth and
memory constraints~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing}.

To alleviate this pixel load, fielded autonomous systems typically use a
two-stage cascade~\cite{fouda2022lightweight}. A first stage (radar priors,
acoustic sensors, or simple motion/background-subtraction algorithms)
identifies candidate Regions of Interest (RoI) in the frame. A second stage
crops these small patches (commonly $64\times64$ or $128\times128$ pixels)
and feeds them to a neural network for verification.

Prior work in this space commonly adapts general-purpose detectors (the
YOLO family, SSD) — originally developed for full-frame optical/aerial
object detection~\cite{li2020object} — to the RoI-verification
task~\cite{achmadiah2025energy,cao2023improved}. We argue, and demonstrate
empirically, that RoI verification in a two-stage system is \emph{not}
simply a scaled-down detection problem. On edge devices, memory bandwidth is
a more critical bottleneck than raw compute (GFLOPs)~\cite{qasaimeh2019comparing};
even edge-compressed YOLO variants (e.g.\ YOLO-Nano) run into resource
limits and energy--performance bottlenecks on FPGA-class
hardware~\cite{adiono2021low,zhang2022apply}. Architectures designed for full-frame
operation, compressed into a micro-input regime, suffer spatial feature loss
from their deep global-pooling stages and asymmetric data flow.

Since total system recall in a two-stage architecture is
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, a
verifier's false negative is a system-level miss. This makes recall, not
mAP, the design-critical metric for the second stage.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) We formalize the RoI-verification problem in
two-stage systems and show empirically that full-frame attention mechanisms
degrade target features in the micro-input regime — with the important
caveat, corrected in this extended version, that the effect is on
\emph{training stability} rather than a uniform accuracy penalty (Section~
\ref{sec:cbam}). (2) We convert the asymmetric background distribution of
candidate regions (mostly empty sky) into a latency advantage via an
early-exit mechanism in the spirit of prior confidence-gated early-exit
classifiers~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,liang2024implementation},
adapted here to the two-stage RoI-verification setting. (3) We identify
and correct a train/test leakage bug
in the RoI dataset construction pipeline that inflated our own preliminary
results, and release the corrected dataset-construction methodology.
(4) We present a controlled, $N=5$-seed comparison — the conference
version's ablation and main results tables were produced under different
training budgets, which this version corrects. (5) We show that decoupling
the bounding-box head from the pooled classification feature (Section~
\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) not only improves localization on its own, but fully
explains away CBAM's originally observed benefit. (6) We measure
cross-sensor (EO$\to$thermal-IR) generalization on the Anti-UAV410
benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}), both zero-shot and via a
thermal-from-scratch reference under the same controlled protocol,
quantifying a real but partial domain-shift gap rather than assuming or
omitting a generalization claim.

\section{EMA-Det: Architecture and Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System-Level Latency Model}\label{sec:latency}
In a two-stage architecture, total system latency $T_{\text{total}}$ is
modeled as the proposal time plus the sum of per-candidate verification
times (Eq.~\ref{eq:latency}).
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{total}} = T_{\text{proposal}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RoI}}} T_{\text{verifier}}^{(i)}
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
When a general-purpose full-frame detector is used as the verifier, every
candidate patch — including empty-sky patches — pays the full network's
fixed cost. EMA-Det's dynamic early-exit structure converts this linear
scaling into an asymptotic one.

\subsection{Laplacian/Sobel-Initialized Kernel Bank}\label{sec:init}
At a constrained $128\times128$ resolution, small targets carry limited
spatial information. Standard Xavier/Kaiming random initialization requires
the network to learn edge boundaries from scratch, increasing iteration cost
and training variance. The first convolution layer's weights $W_1$ are
instead initialized from a deterministic bank of Laplacian ($L$)
\cite{marr1980theory} and Sobel ($S$) \cite{kanopoulos1988design} operators:
\begin{equation}
W^{(0)}_{1,c} = \begin{cases}
K_c, & c \in \{1,\dots,K\} \\
R_c, & c \in \{K+1,\dots,C_{\text{out}}\}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
where $K_c$ are the deterministic physical priors ($L$, $L_2$, $S_x$, $S_y$,
and their signed sums) and $R_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ preserves the
layer's capacity to generalize beyond the fixed operators. These weights are
left unfrozen and are updated during training like any other layer (see
\texttt{ema\_det.py}'s self-test, which verifies both the initial values and
that gradients reach this layer).

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the network. Block~1 (Section~\ref{sec:init})
reduces $1\times128\times128$ to $16\times64\times64$; Blocks~2--4
progressively reduce to $128\times8\times8$; Block~5 fuses two parallel
dilated branches ($d{=}1,2$) to $128\times4\times4$; Block~6 expands to
$256\times4\times4$; a verification head (global average pool, dropout
$0.4$) produces a classification logit, and a separate spatial branch
(Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) produces the 4-coordinate bounding-box
refinement.

CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} was evaluated during architecture development and is
\emph{not} part of the default forward path — Section~\ref{sec:cbam}
presents the corrected, controlled evidence for that decision, which differs
qualitatively from the conference version's ablation.

\subsubsection{Spatial Bounding-Box Head}\label{sec:spatialbbox}
The conference version's box head, like its classification head, operated on
the global-average-pooled Block~6 feature vector — meaning \emph{all}
spatial layout was discarded before box regression ever saw the feature map.
This is a direct, architecturally-obvious explanation for why
mAP@0.5:0.95 lagged so far behind a detector with a spatial box head (Section
\ref{sec:baselines}). We therefore decouple the two heads: classification
stays on the pooled vector (the path whose stability was already validated
across $N{=}5$ seeds, Section~\ref{sec:cbam}), while the box head instead
reads the pre-pooling $256\times4\times4$ feature map directly (flattened,
into a single linear layer). Table~\ref{tab:spatialbbox} reports a
controlled $N{=}5$ ablation: the spatial head improves mAP@0.75
(+5.7 points) and mAP@0.5:0.95 (+2.9 points) at a cost of only 15K
additional parameters (${\sim}1\%$) and negligible FLOPs, while recall is
essentially unchanged and its cross-seed standard deviation actually
\emph{drops} (2.54 $\to$ 0.47). Because it strictly dominates the original
head on every metric we track, it is adopted as the default architecture
throughout this paper; the pooled-vector head remains available
(\texttt{use\_spatial\_bbox=False} in \texttt{ema\_det.py}) for
reproducibility of the original design.

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}
The verification head is optimized with a combined classification and
box-regression loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{p}, p) + \lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}(\hat{b}, b)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}}$ is the Complete-IoU loss~\cite{zheng2020distance},
with $\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$. \textbf{Correction:} the conference draft
stated $\lambda_{\text{box}}=2.5$; the value actually used throughout
training and every ablation (verified directly in the training code) is
$\lambda_{\text{box}}=2.0$. This document uses the code-verified value.
Early-exit gates are supervised with their own BCE terms
($\lambda_{\text{gate1}}=0.3$, $\lambda_{\text{gate2}}=0.4$) against the
same binary label as the main head.

\subsection{Dynamic Early-Exit Gates}\label{sec:gates}
Following the confidence-gated early-exit paradigm
(e.g.\ BranchyNet~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet}), Gates~1 and~2
(after Blocks~2 and~4) implement the asymptotic rule of
Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}: a candidate halts (classified background, no box
refinement) once its confidence falls below $1-\tau$; otherwise it proceeds.
\begin{equation}
\text{Result} = \begin{cases}
\text{Background (halt)}, & \hat{y}_i \le (1-\tau) \\
C_{i+1}(F_i)\ (\text{continue}), & \hat{y}_i > (1-\tau)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset Construction and a Corrected Leakage Bug}\label{sec:leakage}

The dataset is built from two public raw sources, matching the conference
predecessor's data description: rotary-wing and fixed-wing LSS UAV
photographs from a Kaggle collection~\cite{dasmehdixtr_kaggle} (labeled,
80\% of the raw pool), and bird/empty-sky photographs from a Roboflow
Universe collection~\cite{mohsen_roboflow} (unlabeled, hard-negative
source, 20\%). \textbf{This extended version corrects a train/test
leakage bug found in the conference version's dataset-construction
script.}

The original pipeline cropped one positive patch (around each labeled UAV,
with a $3\times$ context factor) and one hard-negative patch (from the same
photograph, near but not overlapping the UAV) per raw image, then pooled
\emph{all} resulting patches from \emph{all} raw photographs and split them
into train/val/test by random shuffle at the \emph{patch} level. Two
problems compound here. First, the raw photograph pool itself was heavily
duplicated: an $8\times8$ average-hash comparison found 52.1\% of raw
labeled-candidate photographs (and 69.1\% of raw sky photographs) have a
bit-identical near-duplicate elsewhere in the same pool — consistent with
sequential video-frame extraction (e.g.\ \texttt{rotor/0032.jpg} and
\texttt{rotor/0033.jpg} hash identically). Second, because splitting
happened at the patch level with no deduplication, near-duplicate source
photographs — and even sibling patches cropped from the very same
photograph — could land on opposite sides of the train/test boundary.
Empirically, 83.8\% of test-set positive (UAV) patches were bit-identical
to a training-set positive patch under the same hash.

We fix this in two steps, implemented in
\texttt{build\_dataset\_clean.py}: (1) deduplicate raw photographs via the
same average-hash (Hamming distance $\le 5$ clustered via union-find, one
representative kept per cluster, preferring a labeled member), reducing
5{,}921 raw labeled-candidate photographs to 2{,}142 unique ones (and 2{,}872
raw sky photographs to 887); (2) split the \emph{deduplicated photograph
pool} into train/val/test (70/15/15) \emph{before} any patch cropping, so
every patch derived from a given photograph is guaranteed to land in that
photograph's assigned split. Re-running the leakage check on the resulting
dataset finds the test-positive exact-duplicate rate drops from 83.8\% to
0.6\% (residual consistent with hash collisions on low-detail images, not
real duplication). As a direct consequence of removing duplicated content,
the dataset shrinks from 23{,}620 patches to 4{,}136 genuinely unique
patches (2{,}842 train / 662 val / 632 test).

We report all results in this paper on this corrected dataset. Numbers are
consequently not directly comparable to the conference version, which
reported inflated metrics due to leakage.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All models are trained with AdamW, 5-epoch linear warmup followed by cosine
annealing, batch size 16, gradient clipping at norm 5.0. \textbf{Correction:}
the conference draft stated weight decay $=0.01$; the code-verified value
used throughout is $\text{weight\_decay}=10^{-4}$, which is what this
document uses. Early stopping uses patience 20 on validation mAP@0.5, for
up to 150 epochs. \textbf{All reported results use $N=5$ seeds
$\{42, 7, 123, 2024, 31415\}$ under this identical protocol} — including the
CBAM ablation, which in the conference version was run at a much smaller
budget ($N=1$, 50 epochs) than the main comparison table, confounding the
effect of CBAM with the effect of training budget. This version removes
that confound.

Augmentation excludes Mosaic/MixUp (destroys small-target shape at
$128\times128$); we use horizontal flip, brightness/contrast jitter,
directional motion blur, Gaussian noise, small-angle rotation, and coarse
dropout.

This result changes the CBAM story again, and for the better: once the box
head retains spatial information (Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}), CBAM's
entire localization advantage \emph{disappears}. mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75, and
mAP@0.5:0.95 are statistically indistinguishable between the two
configurations (differences well within one combined standard deviation).
What CBAM still costs is recall: $3$ points lower on average
($93.23\pm1.37\%$ vs.\ $96.08\pm0.47\%$) and moderately less stable across
seeds (std $1.37$ vs.\ $0.47$) — a real but far milder effect than the
catastrophic instability observed under the legacy (pooled-vector) box head,
where 2 of 5 seeds collapsed to 74--79\% recall (std $8.85$). No seed
collapses under the spatial-bbox head; the worst CBAM run here still reaches
90.8\% recall.

Read together with Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}, this suggests CBAM's
originally observed mAP benefit was never really about attention — it was
compensating for spatial information the pooled-vector box head had
discarded. Once that information is restored directly (and reliably, at
negligible parameter/FLOPs cost) via the spatial bbox head, CBAM has nothing
left to contribute on localization, and only its (now smaller, but still
present) recall cost remains. This is a materially different — and more
mechanistically satisfying — explanation than either of our two earlier
analyses offered: not a training-budget confound (our first correction),
and not simply ``attention destabilizes training in this regime'' (our
second), but that CBAM and the spatial bbox head were solving the same
underlying problem, and only one of them does so without a reliability cost.
On this basis, the final architecture omits CBAM.

This table is the clearest empirical support for Section~\ref{sec:intro}'s central claim.
YOLOv8n — a full detection architecture with a proper multi-scale head and

But YOLOv8n's recall is capped at
$73.75\pm1.86\%$ (tight variance across seeds — this is a consistent
property of the architecture on this task, not seed noise), well below
every other model in this table. Given
$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{proposal}} \times R_{\text{verifier}}$, this
$\sim$23-point recall deficit is exactly the failure mode this paper argues
against: a verifier optimized for (and evaluated by) mAP can look
substantially stronger on the metric authors typically report while being
a \emph{worse} second-stage verifier in the metric that actually determines
system-level performance. The two lightweight backbones (MobileNetV3-Small,
ShuffleNetV2 x0.5), trained from scratch with no architectural changes beyond
a 1-channel first layer and the same verification head as EMA-Det, land close
to EMA-Det on both mAP and recall despite having 40--77\% fewer parameters —
they are competitive generic backbones for this task, but neither offers
EMA-Det's early-exit compute savings (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}) or its
recall stability advantage over CBAM (Section~\ref{sec:cbam}).

\subsection{Early-Exit Trade-off}\label{sec:earlyexit}

Table~\ref{tab:earlyexit} reports the $\tau$ sweep for the final (no-CBAM)
model on the corrected dataset. Because the clean test set no longer
contains patches the model has effectively memorized (Section
\ref{sec:leakage}), Gate~1's rejection rate at $\tau{=}0.85$ is honestly
lower than the conference draft's (24.8\% vs.\ the previously reported
34.4\%) — the remaining candidates are genuinely harder, not artificially

\subsection{Hardware Validation Status}\label{sec:hardware}

\textbf{This section reports only what has actually been measured or
built; no synthesis numbers are fabricated or estimated.} Both
configurations (final and CBAM, spatial-bbox architecture,
Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) were exported to ONNX and verified
bit-for-bit-close against their PyTorch models (max abs.\ difference
$1.7\times10^{-5}$ / $1.3\times10^{-5}$ over 20 random inputs). On the
development machine (RTX~4060 laptop GPU + host CPU — not Jetson/FPGA),
single-sample PyTorch-CUDA inference reaches 378.7 FPS (final) / 248.8 FPS
(CBAM); ONNX Runtime's CPU execution provider reaches 738.1 / 646.5 FPS
respectively (this development environment's onnxruntime-gpu build could
not load its CUDA/TensorRT provider libraries — a local dependency issue,
not a claim about GPU-accelerated ONNX performance in general). The spatial
bbox head's extra flatten+linear layer costs a real, measured slowdown
versus the legacy head's earlier-measured 465.0 FPS (final) / 262.6 FPS
(CBAM) — worth noting honestly alongside the accuracy gain, not just the
gain alone.

A literal C++ translation of the Block1$\to$Gate1$\to$Block4$\to$Gate2
path, using the actual trained (BatchNorm-folded) weights and a testbench
validating it against PyTorch reference outputs on real test images, has
been prepared for Vivado/Vitis HLS synthesis targeting a Zynq-7020 (see
\texttt{hardware/}). \textbf{It has not been compiled, simulated, or
synthesized} — this development environment has no C/C++ toolchain and no
Vivado/Vitis HLS installation. LUT, BRAM, latency, and power figures
require running the prepared \texttt{run\_hls.tcl} flow on a machine with
the toolchain, plus resolving a known design gap: a static
element-count$\times$bit-width tally of the current kernel's per-layer
buffers (\texttt{src/estimate\_hls\_bram.py}, not a synthesis result) sums
to 1688~KB at the working \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,6>} precision against the
Zynq-7020's 630~KB total BRAM ($2.68\times$ over budget), with
Block~1/Block~2 alone accounting for 60.7\% of that total —
prioritizing an AXI4-Stream/line-buffer restructuring of those two blocks
first is informed by this arithmetic, though the real fix must still be
driven by actual \texttt{csynth} resource reports rather than estimated in
advance. We report this status transparently rather than presenting
placeholder numbers as measurements.

\subsection{Cross-Sensor Generalization}\label{sec:crosssensor}

To probe how much of EMA-Det's EO-domain performance transfers to an
unseen sensor modality, we built a leakage-free RoI verification dataset
from the Anti-UAV410 thermal-IR benchmark~\cite{antiuav410}, which ships
its own video-level train/val/test split (each video's frames stay
together). One video (\texttt{02\_6319}) was found split across the
train and test partitions under non-overlapping frame ranges and was
excluded from both out of caution. Positive patches were cropped around
the annotated drone box (context multiplier $3\times$, resized to
$128\times128$); negatives combine the rare drone-absent
("$0,0,0,0$") frames with same-frame hard negatives cropped near the
drone but not overlapping it, mirroring the EO pipeline's negative-mining
strategy (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Every 15th frame of each video was
sampled to avoid near-duplicate adjacent frames. The resulting dataset has
27{,}768 / 12{,}388 / 16{,}808 train/val/test patches.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer.} The EO-trained ``final'' checkpoint
(spatial-bbox, no CBAM, Section~\ref{sec:spatialbbox}) was evaluated
directly on the thermal test/val splits with no fine-tuning of any kind.
Table~\ref{tab:crosssensor} reports the result. Precision stays high
(95.3--95.5\%), but recall falls from the EO in-domain
$96.08\pm0.47\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:cbam}) to 80--86\% on thermal data, and
mAP@0.5 drops to 39--52\%, evidencing a real but partial generalization
gap: the verifier's Laplacian/Sobel-initialized first layer and learned
features carry over a meaningful UAV/non-UAV discriminative signal across
modalities without ever seeing a thermal frame, but a UAV silhouette in
thermal-IR is visually different enough from the EO training distribution
that a non-trivial fraction of true positives are missed.

\textbf{Thermal-from-scratch reference.} To give the zero-shot number an
upper bound to be read against, we trained EMA-Det (final config: no
CBAM, spatial-bbox head) from scratch on the thermal train/val split under
the identical $N=5$-seed / 150-epoch / early-stopping protocol used
throughout this paper. Table~\ref{tab:thermalscratch} reports the result:
mAP@0.5 $89.52\pm0.93\%$, recall $91.64\pm0.41\%$, precision
$97.55\pm0.39\%$ — all comfortably above both the zero-shot transfer
numbers and the EO in-domain result itself. Two things follow. First, the
zero-shot gap in Table~\ref{tab:crosssensor} is a genuine domain-shift
effect, not a ceiling imposed by the architecture or a self-inflicted
protocol difference: the same network, same parameter count
(1{,}532{,}391), and same FLOPs (423.085M) reach 89.5\% mAP@0.5 when
allowed to actually learn the thermal domain. Second, thermal-IR UAV
verification at this resolution and context window appears to be an
\emph{easier} task than the EO equivalent — thermal drones present as a
comparatively clean, high-contrast blob against a more uniform background,
whereas EO photography carries much more background clutter and
appearance variation — which is consistent with the higher mAP/precision
achieved here despite recall being marginally below the EO in-domain
number.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dataset size.} Deduplication reduced the corrected EO dataset
to 4{,}136 patches (from 23{,}620 pre-deduplication) — a much smaller
training set than the conference version used, which may itself limit both
absolute performance and how tightly seed variance can be controlled.
Expanding the raw source pool with additional, verified-non-duplicate UAV
photography is future work.
\item \textbf{Cross-sensor generalization.} Section~\ref{sec:crosssensor}
reports both a zero-shot EO$\to$thermal transfer number and a
thermal-from-scratch reference; both are single-dataset (Anti-UAV410)
results, and no attempt at domain adaptation or multi-sensor joint
training was made — a natural next step given the from-scratch result
shows a well-trained verifier reaches high accuracy on thermal data.
\item \textbf{Hardware validation.} See Section~\ref{sec:hardware} — FPGA
synthesis is prepared but not executed in this draft.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This extended version corrects three compounding methodological issues in
our conference report: a CBAM/training-budget confound, a train/test
leakage bug traced to undeduplicated, patch-level-split source photographs,
and a bounding-box head that discarded spatial information before
regression. Correcting the first two and then the third in sequence
re-establishes EMA-Det's results under a controlled, $N=5$-seed protocol on
a verified leakage-free dataset, and reveals that the third correction fully
explains away CBAM's originally observed benefit: it was compensating for
information the legacy box head had discarded, not adding anything unique.
The corrected picture is more nuanced than originally reported, and more
mechanistically satisfying than either intermediate correction alone. We
view publishing this chain of corrections, together with the tooling that
found each issue (\texttt{check\_leakage.py},
\texttt{check\_raw\_duplicates.py}), as itself a contribution for others
building similar two-stage RoI-verification datasets from web-aggregated
source imagery.
